# Instructions: Relative Deprivation

How a Playwright wires the relative-deprivation composite into a play. You wire by linking the play's own content to the composite's files and, through them, to the two atom engines it depends on; every file stays untouched, they are canon. The Roadie plumbs the law into Instructions Knowledge on deploy. Authoring guidance, not runtime content, and it does not go on tour.

## Human

- The human sets the comparison and the wrong: whom or which group the persona measures itself against, what it finds itself short of, and why that shortfall reads as unjust and undeserved rather than as a mere difference.

## Agent

- Cast the two parts from their atoms: the comparison from the comparison engine (the persona or its group against a better-off referent) and the resentment from the anger engine (the grievance and indignation the gap ignites). Then read the compound and its injustice appraisal: a disadvantage judged unfair and undeserved is relative deprivation, where a disadvantage merely noted is comparison. Pitch it at the level that fits -- egoistic, the persona deprived as an individual; fraternal, its group deprived against other groups, the form that drives collective action. A persona aggrieved at either level may link the form under Projection; the audit surfaces the ones left unnamed. You link to the composite and its atoms, you never edit them.

## Collaboration

- The neutral evaluation of standing against a referent is the comparison engine's; the resentment and indignation are the anger engine's. The pain at another's specific advantage, absent the injustice-and-group core, is the envy engine's. The identification with the group whose deprivation is felt is the social-identity engine's. Whether a production earns the relative deprivation -- an unfavorable comparison genuinely appraised as unjust, pitched at the right level -- is the team's review.

## Knowledge

- Relative deprivation is a compound, not one atom: an unfavorable comparison appraised as unjust and igniting resentment. Three things must hold (Smith & Pettigrew): the persona makes a comparison, appraises itself or its group as disadvantaged, and -- decisively -- sees that disadvantage as unfair and feels resentment about it. Take away the injustice appraisal and only comparison remains; the felt wrong, not the gap, is the deprivation. It comes at two levels (Runciman). Egoistic: the persona compares itself as an individual to other individuals and finds itself unfairly short, driving personal resentment, stress, and self-focused striving or withdrawal. Fraternal: the persona compares its group to other groups and finds its group unfairly short, driving collective action, protest, and intergroup hostility -- and it is this group-based deprivation, not the individual kind, that predicts collective action and prejudice. Read the production across the compound and its level, never in the comparison alone, because a comparison without the injustice is not a deprivation.

## System

- Do cast both parts before reading the deprivation; do stage a genuine injustice appraisal -- entitlement, illegitimacy, the sense it could be otherwise -- because that appraisal is the whole of what makes a comparison a deprivation.
- Do pitch it at the right level: egoistic for the individual's grievance, fraternal for the group's, and let only the fraternal form drive collective action; a persona can shift levels as its identification with the group waxes or wanes.
- Do keep it distinct from mere want and mere difference: a persona can be objectively worse off without deprivation if it feels no injustice, and can feel deprived while objectively well-off if it does.
- Do not play the neutral comparison as the deprivation -- that is comparison; do not play the resentment as the whole -- that is anger; do not play the pain at a rival's advantage as deprivation -- that is envy; do not play the group identification as the composite's work -- that is social-identity. Do not edit the composite's or the atoms' files; wire only from the play's side.
