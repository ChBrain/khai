# References

## Line of Work

The composite models hoarding following the cognitive-behavioral framework formalized by Frost and Hartl (1996), which separated hoarding from obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Hoarding is represented as a multi-component syndrome: information processing deficits (categorization and decision making), emotional attachment to possessions (extended-self), erroneous beliefs about the nature of possessions (scarcity), and behavioral avoidance (coping). The excessive acquisition that characterizes a large majority of cases is modeled using the collection atom. The model is decoupled from OCD, reflecting the DSM-5 change (2013) that established hoarding disorder as a distinct diagnosis, a move supported by evidence that hoarding often lacks ego-dystonic obsessions and responds poorly to standard OCD treatments (Mataix-Cols et al., 2010).

## Restrictions

- **Collection**: The `collection` engine models ordered, bounded, and ego-syntonic acquisition. Hoarding is disorganized, unbounded, and often functionally impairing.
- **Categorization**: The general mechanism for concept formation belongs to `categorization`. The composite relies on the deficit in this mechanism (where every item is seen as unique).
- **Extended Self**: The mechanism of investing identity in objects belongs to `extended-self`.
- **Scarcity**: The belief in future need or utility belongs to `scarcity`.
- **Decision**: The general mechanism for choosing belongs to `decision`. The composite uses the paralysis specifically related to discarding.
- **Coping**: Avoidance as a general strategy belongs to `coping`.
- **Diagnosis**: This is authored persona-architecture for a fictional play. It is never a clinical assessment or diagnosis of a real person.
