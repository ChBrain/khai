# Heeding: Reference

## Line of Work

Heeding as **composite**: what a persona gives a rule they did not agree with, and what buys it. It
is not a fourth mechanism but a wiring of three. The **law** engine supplies what is reaching the
persona -- a rule that binds because another rule says so, met not as a text but at the gap, through
the discretion of whoever is applying it. The **recognition** engine supplies the encounter -- a claim
for acknowledgment answered by somebody with the standing to answer it, settling into a standing the
persona then holds. The **moral-conviction** engine supplies the stamp: whether the question at issue
is held as a preference or as a mandate, objective and owing nothing to any authority.

The joining is Tyler's, and it is in none of the three. Deterrence research had asked what makes
sanctions work; Tyler asked why people obey the law when nobody is watching and the sanction is
remote, and found that the answer is not about sanctions at all. Across a panel of Chicago residents
followed through their actual encounters with police and courts, what predicted compliance,
acceptance of decisions, and later cooperation was the **perceived fairness of the process** rather
than the favourability of the outcome, and it held for people who lost. Lind and Tyler supplied the
reason it works: a procedure is relational information. A persona has no independent way to learn what
they are worth to an institution, so how its officials handle them when something is at stake is the
best evidence available -- which makes voice, neutrality, respect, and evident good faith a reading
about standing rather than a reading about the case.

That is why the composite needs recognition as an atom and not merely law. Tyler's four elements are
not four courtesies; three of the four are about being treated as somebody who counts, and the
mechanism runs through the encounter and the standing it returns -- which is the recognition engine's
sequence exactly, arriving through an institution rather than through a person. And it is why the
composite reads the treatment and never the ruling: the ruling is not what moved them.

Hence the twist, which is Skitka's, and which is the reason moral-conviction is the third atom. Where
a persona holds the question at issue with **moral conviction**, the mechanism reverses. They judge
the procedure by the answer it reached rather than the answer by the procedure that reached it, so
fair process stops predicting acceptance and outcome takes over entirely -- and the reversal runs both
ways, since a persona whose mandate is vindicated will accept the result from a process they would
otherwise call rigged. The moral-conviction engine already carries the general form of this, that
authority and procedure are subordinated to the mandate. What the composite adds is the specific,
costly consequence for a legal order: the cheapest and most reliable instrument an institution has for
producing compliance is exactly the instrument that fails on the questions people care most about.

Hence the keystone. A fair hearing buys compliance only on the questions a persona does not already
consider settled, and on the ones they do, the fairness makes the loss **worse**. A scrupulous process
that reaches the wrong answer removes the last available explanation for the loss: a rigged process
leaves the persona a way to hold the institution mistaken, and a fair one leaves them nothing to
conclude except that it is complicit. Which is why an institution's best behaviour is its most
dangerous behaviour on precisely the cases it most wants to settle.

It is the first composite to wire the law engine, and it deliberately carries the account law itself
delegates rather than owns -- law states the condition, and this reads what a persona does inside it.
It is not the first to wire recognition or moral-conviction, and it owns neither: an atom carries a
phenomenon, and a composite carries a question asked of it.

The domain does not model either side alone -- what makes a rule a rule and the gap between declared
and applied are law's; the claim, encounter, and standing sequence is recognition's; the mandate stamp
is moral-conviction's. Nor is it obedience to a command against one's own conscience, which is
obedience's and runs on an authority in the room; nor the person made by institutional power, which is
subjection's; nor what a persona holds another to have coming, which is deserving's. It owns the
purchase: compliance bought with treatment, and the boundary where the currency stops being accepted.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** The joining is a real and unusually well-supported result, and
the twist is a real and narrower one, but the composite is an inference across three atoms and should
be read as one. Procedural justice is among the better-replicated findings in social psychology and
has held across courts, policing, tax authorities, workplaces, and schools, in field studies as well
as surveys, and its central surprise -- that people who lost accept the loss when the process treated
them well -- reproduces reliably. What is **weaker is the causal direction and the size**. Much of the
foundational work is cross-sectional or based on recalled encounters, so a persona who is disposed to
regard the institution favourably may both remember the process as fairer and comply more; the
randomised procedural-justice interventions that have tested the loop directly give real but
considerably smaller effects than the correlational literature implies, and several have failed to
replicate at scale. The relational explanation -- that procedure works because it carries standing
information -- is well argued and is not the only candidate; a simpler account, that fair procedure is
evidence of accurate procedure, predicts much of the same data and the composite does not exclude it.
Skitka's moral-mandate reversal is a genuine and repeatedly demonstrated effect, but the studies are
predominantly experimental and vignette-based, run on a small number of highly moralised issues, and
how far it generalises to an ordinary persona's ordinary encounters is not established. What should
not be taken from the composite is that fair treatment is a substitute for just outcomes, or that a
persona refusing a fair verdict is being irrational. The claim is narrow: expect treatment to do far
more work than the ruling, expect that to be cheap for an institution and spendable, and expect it to
buy nothing at all on a question the persona holds as already answered.

## Origin

Procedural justice and the psychology of moral mandates, wired as a composite over the law,
recognition, and moral-conviction engines. The first row is the **joining** -- the published work that
makes the fairness of the process, rather than the outcome, the thing that purchases compliance -- and
the rest anchor the individual bridges and their bounds.

## Restrictions

What the composite refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The three sides (the atoms)**: The composite wires the three but owns none. What makes a rule a
  rule, the eight ways a rule system stops being law, the three standings toward legality, and the gap
  between declared and applied are the law engine's. The claim, the encounter, and the standing are
  recognition's. The mandate stamp and its mobilizing and intolerant faces are moral-conviction's.
  Heeding reads only what the first two do to each other, and where the third stops them.
- **Obeying a command against one's conscience (obedience)**: Milgram's destructive obedience, and
  compliance with an authority present and instructing, is the obedience engine's. Heeding's persona
  is not being commanded -- they have been decided about, usually by an institution rather than a
  person, and frequently after everybody has gone home. That the two are separate is the reason this
  composite exists: the law engine delegates the question of why a persona complies, and this is the
  answer that runs through treatment rather than through command.
- **The person made by institutional power (subjection)**: The world handed ready-made, the conduct
  trained by watching, and the self surrendered to a command or an enclosing place are the subjection
  composite's. Heeding runs on a single encounter and its purchase; subjection runs on what a lifetime
  of them makes of somebody. A play may hold both, and should not confuse a persona who complied
  because they were treated well with one who no longer experiences the grip as a grip.
- **What somebody has coming (deserving, condemnation, moral-account)**: Moral judgment, desert,
  costly sanction, the CAD triad, and the ledgers of guilt, repair, and forgiveness are those
  composites'. Heeding is indifferent to whether the persona was in fact right, and its persona is not
  owed anything by anybody -- they are deciding whether to go along.
- **The strength of the norms in force (tightness-looseness)**: How strict a society's norms are and
  what latitude it grants for deviance is Gelfand's and that engine's. Heeding's subject is one
  persona's transaction with one institution, and it runs the same way in tight and loose worlds.
- **Trust in another's reliability (trust)**: The bet on somebody whose conduct cannot be controlled
  -- extended, tested, betrayed, repaired -- is the trust engine's. Heeding reads a persona's estimate
  of an institution's motives only as one of four elements in a single encounter, and never as a
  relationship.
- **The refusal as authored virtue (the play's pitch)**: A persona's refusal to accept a fair verdict
  made to **stand for** integrity, or their compliance made to stand for docility, is refused: that is
  authored key, the play's pitch. Heeding sets what the treatment bought and where it stopped buying;
  the meaning read into it is the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A composite root over three engine atoms it declares as dependencies, with
three bridges.

- **[heeding](process_heeding.md)** (the root): what a persona gives a rule they did not agree with,
  read on the encounter and never on the ruling. Anchored by Tyler (1990).
- **[the hearing](process_heeding_hearing.md)** (bridge): the encounter with the machinery, read for
  voice, a neutral decider, ordinary respect, and evident good faith -- none of them about the
  outcome -- returning a settled sense of where the persona stands. Anchored by Lind & Tyler (1988)
  and Thibaut & Walker (1975).
- **[the deference](process_heeding_deference.md)** (bridge): what that reading buys -- obedience
  unwatched, acceptance of an adverse decision, cooperation next time -- tracking the hearing far more
  than the ruling. Anchored by Tyler (1990) and Sunshine & Tyler (2003).
- **[the mandate](process_heeding_mandate.md)** (bridge): the question held as objective and owing
  nothing to authority, on which the persona judges the procedure by its answer instead of the answer
  by its procedure. Anchored by Skitka & Houston (2001) and Skitka (2002); the composite's twist and
  keystone, that a fair hearing buys compliance only on the questions a persona does not already
  consider settled, and on the ones they do the fairness makes the loss worse.
