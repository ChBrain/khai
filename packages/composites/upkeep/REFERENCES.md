# Upkeep: Reference

## Line of Work

Upkeep as **composite**: what a shared thing costs the people who keep it. It is not a fourth
mechanism but a wiring of three: the **commons** engine supplies the resource and the institutions its
own users craft around it, the **trust** engine supplies the bet each persona places on the others,
and the **altruistic-punishment** engine supplies the enforcement and the fact that somebody funds it
personally.

The joining is Ostrom, Walker and Gardner's. Their title states it -- covenants with and without a
sword -- and the experiment tests it: put a common-pool resource in a laboratory, let the users talk,
and give them a way to sanction each other, and the resource survives. Neither element alone does as
much. Communication without sanctioning decays as the agreement is tested; sanctioning without
communication is applied badly. The finding that matters for this composite is what the subjects did
with the sanctioning option: they used it at their own expense, in one-shot conditions, recovering
nothing. The covenant was not self-executing, and the sword had to be paid for.

That is why the composite needs all three atoms. From the commons: Ostrom's design principles --
boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, rules the bound can change -- which are genuinely good and
genuinely work, so this composite starts from a success rather than from a tragedy. What the design
does not specify is who bears the cost of executing it. From trust: what actually sets the
contribution. Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr found that most participants are neither unconditional
cooperators nor free-riders but conditional -- they contribute in proportion to what they expect
others to contribute -- which makes the arrangement rest on an estimate, and makes a single visible
defection revise everybody's estimate at once rather than merely removing one share. From altruistic
punishment: the answer, and its price. Fehr and Gächter showed cooperation collapsing without a
punishment option and holding with one, and that punishers pay for it with no material return.

The last is the composite's twist, and it is the second-order problem Yamagishi named. The
enforcement has the same structure as the thing it protects: its benefit falls on everybody and its
cost on whoever carries it out, so a persona can free-ride on the punishing exactly as they could on
the pool. Nothing in the arrangement obliges anyone to pick it up, and it is never anyone's turn.

Hence the keystone. Because the cost is private and the benefit is general, a commons that is working
presents as one that needs no keeping -- the sanctions look unnecessary precisely when they are doing
their job, and the only people positioned to see the cost are the ones paying it. Ostrom spent a
career on the consequence: local institutions that had held for centuries dismantled by outside
authorities who could not see what was holding them.

It is the third composite to wire the trust engine and the second to wire altruistic punishment.
Dealing runs trust on a bargain between two parties and vigil on a bond with a rival near it; here it
runs on a group, where the bet is placed on people in general rather than on one counterparty.
Deserving runs altruistic punishment as moral desert -- what somebody has coming for what they did --
where this runs it as the fee for a shared thing, paid by whoever is willing. Same engines, different
questions, and no composite owns them.

The domain does not model any side alone -- the pool, the boundary, and the sanction are the commons
engine's; extend, test, withhold, betrayed, and repair are trust's; second-party and third-party
punishment are altruistic punishment's. Nor is it who counts as us, which is membership's and
intergroup's; nor the group's own working and its safety to speak, which is airing's; nor how a thing
spreads through a population, which is adoption's. It owns the keeping: who pays, and what it costs
them.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** The atoms are well founded and the joining is a robust
experimental result, but the punishment leg carries two findings that complicate it and both belong
here. First, punishment is not universally constructive: Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter ran the same
public-goods game with punishment across many societies and found substantial **antisocial**
punishment in several of them -- cooperators sanctioned by free-riders -- and where that happened,
punishment did not raise cooperation and sometimes destroyed it. The mechanism is not a civilising
device that can be dropped into any group. Second, punishment often does not pay: Dreber, Rand,
Fudenberg and Nowak found that in a repeated game the highest-scoring players were the ones who did
not punish at all, since the costs of sanctioning consumed the gains it produced. Both findings are
consistent with the composite's own claim -- the sword is expensive and its bearer is not compensated
-- but they rule out reading it as an endorsement. Ostrom's field evidence is observational and
selects on institutions that survived, which is a real limitation on inferring what makes them
survive. What should not be taken from this composite is that shared things are doomed without
punishment, or that punishing is the virtuous move. The claim is structural: where a shared thing is
being kept, expect the contribution to track an estimate rather than a rule, expect the estimate to
need visible answering, and expect the answering to be paid for privately by somebody who is not
reimbursed.

## Origin

The study of common-pool resources and the experimental economics of cooperation and punishment,
wired as a composite over the commons, trust, and altruistic-punishment engines. The first row is the
**joining** -- the published work that puts a common-pool institution and costly sanctioning into one
design and measures what happens -- and the rest anchor the individual bridges and their bounds.

## Restrictions

What the composite refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The three sides (the atoms)**: The composite wires the three but owns none. The pool, the
  boundary, and the sanction are the commons engine's; extend, test, withhold, betrayed, and repair
  are trust's; second-party and third-party punishment are altruistic punishment's. Upkeep reads only
  what the three make together, and one alone is a place or a process, not the keeping.
- **The bargain between two (dealing)**: The dealing composite wires this same trust engine on a
  negotiation, where the vulnerability is contractual and each side is protecting a position. Upkeep's
  bet is placed on people in general and on nobody in particular, and there is nothing to trade.
- **A bond with a rival near it (vigil)**: The vigil composite wires this same trust engine on one
  relationship under threat from a third party. Upkeep has no bond and no rival; its persona is
  estimating a population.
- **What somebody has coming for what they did (deserving)**: The deserving composite wires this same
  altruistic-punishment engine as moral desert -- the judgement, the sanction, and the pleasure at a
  fall. Upkeep runs it as the running cost of a shared thing, and its punisher may feel nothing in
  particular about the defector. A play may run both on one persona, and should not confuse a fee
  with a verdict.
- **Who counts as us (membership, intergroup)**: Social identity, conformity, and the us/them line
  are those composites'. The commons boundary is about who may draw, which is not the same question
  as who belongs, and a play that runs them together will lose both.
- **The group's own working (airing)**: Psychological safety, the group process, and the org are the
  airing composite's. Upkeep is not about whether the group functions well; it is about what the
  shared thing costs.
- **How a thing spreads (adoption)**: Diffusion through a population -- innovators, the chasm, the
  old ground -- is the adoption engine's. Upkeep concerns a thing already in place and being drawn
  on.
- **A place's own life (wildland, neighborhood-cycle)**: Fire, water, succession, dereliction, and
  reclamation are those composites', and they run on the land rather than on the people. Upkeep reads
  the personas around a resource, not the resource's own regime.
- **The collapse as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A shared thing failing in order to **stand
  for** a theme -- the dry well that mirrors a motif of neglect -- is refused: that is authored key,
  the play's pitch, not the force. Upkeep sets who was paying; the meaning read into the failure is
  the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A composite root over three engine atoms it declares as dependencies, with
three bridges.

- **[upkeep](process_upkeep.md)** (the root): what a shared thing costs the people who keep it, read
  on who is paying and never on whether the rule is right. Anchored by Ostrom, Walker & Gardner
  (1992).
- **[the pool](process_upkeep_pool.md)** (bridge): the subtractable resource and the boundaries,
  monitoring, and graduated sanctions its own users crafted -- an arrangement that works and that no
  part of runs by itself. Anchored by Ostrom (1990) and Hardin (1968).
- **[the read](process_upkeep_read.md)** (bridge): the contribution set by what each persona expects
  the others to do, so the arrangement rests on an estimate and a visible defection revises everyone's
  at once. Anchored by Fischbacher, Gächter & Fehr (2001).
- **[the sword](process_upkeep_sword.md)** (bridge): the sanction carried out by somebody who pays
  for it and receives nothing. Anchored by Fehr & Gächter (2000, 2002) and Yamagishi (1986); the
  composite's twist and keystone, that because the cost is private and the benefit general, a commons
  that is working looks like one that needs no keeping.
