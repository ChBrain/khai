# Dealing: Reference

## Line of Work

Dealing as **composite**: two parties who cannot get what they want without each other and can
each impose costs. It is not a fourth mechanism but a wiring of three: the **negotiation** engine
supplies the pursuit of terms, the **trust** engine supplies the exposure accepted on conduct that
cannot be controlled, and the **escalation** engine supplies the loop neither party is steering.
This composite adds no fourth engine; it reads the seam the three make -- that these are not three
relationships but three states of one, and a pair in this condition is always in exactly one of
them -- and stages that seam into three bridges, closing on the discovery that everything each
side does to make itself safe is what puts the agreement out of reach.

The domain does not model any single state -- preparation, exploration, invention, and resolution
are the negotiation engine's; extending, testing, withholding, betrayal, and repair are trust's;
hardening, the antagonist, and retaliation are escalation's; and one alone is a process, not the
condition the pair are in. Nor is it the moral wrong between two and its reckoning, which is the
moral-account composite's; nor the settlement of accounts that ends a relation, which is owing's;
nor how the talk itself works, which is crosstalk's and utterance's. It owns the mixed-motive
relation: what two parties who need each other and can hurt each other end up with, and why it is
so seldom what was available.

The spine is Deutsch, and half the join is already declared inside one of the atoms -- the trust
engine anchors his 1958 paper on trust and suspicion, where trust is set out as a choice to expose
oneself under uncertainty in a mixed-motive game rather than as a sentiment about somebody. His
later work supplies the rest and the claim the composite turns on: cooperative and competitive
processes are self-reinforcing, each tending to elicit more of itself, so the character of the
process a pair falls into does more to determine their outcome than the substance they are
dividing. With Krauss he gave that its sharpest demonstration: in a bargaining game where each
party could obstruct the other, having the means to threaten made both worse off, and both having
it was worst of all -- players with no capacity to hurt each other earned the most. The leverage
acquired to strengthen a hand is what costs the hand. That result is the composite's keystone in
experimental form, and it is why the third bridge is not a story about temper.

Pruitt, Rubin and Kim supply the other flank, and it is likewise already inside an atom -- the
escalation engine anchors their account of the transformations that drive a spiral. What their
book carries beyond that, and what this composite wires, is that escalation, stalemate, and
settlement are one arc rather than separate subjects, with a pair moving between contending,
yielding, problem-solving, and avoiding according to how much concern each holds for their own
outcome and for the other's. That is the negotiation engine and the escalation engine described by
a single model, which is the joining this composite needed. Fisher and Ury add the practical
statement of what the table can find when it looks -- interests under positions, options invented
rather than divided -- and Raiffa and Mnookin add the reason it so often does not: the negotiator's
dilemma, in which the candour that creates value is the same candour that can be used to claim it,
so no party can pursue both wholeheartedly at once.

What the composite adds on top of that join is the single arithmetic running through all three
states. At the table, a position held firmly protects a share and narrows the ground an agreement
could stand on. In the bet, an exposure refused removes a risk and turns every term into something
that must be verified, drafted, escrowed, and enforced, until a deal worth doing is too expensive
to write down. In the spiral, a capacity to hurt the other party is acquired and both parties do
worse for it. Three different instruments of self-protection, three different mechanisms, one
result: each is paid for out of the agreement itself. The three bridges are the table, the bet,
and the spiral.

## Origin

Conflict resolution, the trust literature, and negotiation analysis, wired as a composite over the
negotiation, trust, and escalation engines. The first two rows are the **joining** -- the published
work that sets bargaining, exposure, and escalation as states of one relation -- and the rest
anchor the individual bridges.

## Restrictions

What the composite refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The three states (the atoms)**: The composite wires the three but owns none. Preparation,
  exploration, invention, and resolution are the negotiation engine's; extending, testing,
  withholding, betrayal, and repair are trust's; hardening, the antagonist, and retaliation are
  escalation's. Dealing reads only what the three share -- a mixed-motive pair, always in one of
  them -- and one alone is a process rather than the condition.
- **The wrong and its reckoning (moral-account)**: A betrayal is two things at once and the split
  matters. The felt guilt, the grievance, the account demanded, the forgiveness given or refused
  are the moral-account composite's, which owns the **injury**; this owns the **price**, which is
  what the relation now costs to run. A pair can settle the moral question completely and still
  have a deal that has become unaffordable, and can do the reverse.
- **The account that ends a relation (owing)**: The gift, the debt, and the money -- and what
  settling does to a bond -- are the owing composite's. Dealing reads a relation that is still
  live because neither party can get what they want alone; owing reads what happens when the
  account can finally be closed.
- **How the talk works (crosstalk, utterance, rapport)**: Accommodation, framing, grounding, the
  speech act and its implicature, and the felt quality of a connection working belong to those
  composites. Dealing is indifferent to how well two parties are communicating: pairs who
  understand each other perfectly spiral, and the composite's claim is about structure, not
  mutual comprehension.
- **What is withheld as truth (opacity)**: The secret, the disclosure, and the deception are
  opacity's, read as truth-status. Dealing reads exposure as an instrument with a price, so a party
  who conceals is doing something opacity models and something this composite prices, and the two
  readings are separate.
- **The moral verdict and the standing rank (deserving, standing)**: What a party holds the other
  to have coming is deserving's; the capital, hierarchy, power, and status either holds is
  standing's. This composite reads only the leverage in the deal at hand, which may run entirely
  against the standing rank.
- **What happens between groups (intergroup)**: Aggression, bias, and social identity between
  groups are the intergroup composite's. Dealing reads parties, which may be two people or two
  sides with agents, and it does not model what belonging to a side does to anybody.
- **The breakdown as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A collapse made to **stand for** a theme
  -- the talks that fail to mirror a motif of division -- is refused: that is authored key, the
  play's pitch, not the force on the pair. Dealing sets the state; the meaning read into it is the
  play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A composite root over three engine atoms it declares as dependencies, with
three bridges, ordered by what each side has put at risk.

- **[dealing](process_dealing.md)** (the root): two parties who cannot get what they want without
  each other and can each impose costs, read on the pair rather than on either persona. Anchored by
  Deutsch (1973) on self-reinforcing processes and Pruitt, Rubin & Kim (1994) on the single arc.
- **[the table](process_dealing_table.md)** (bridge): the bargaining itself, where the ground an
  agreement could stand on is widened by finding interests and narrowed by committing to positions.
  Anchored by Fisher & Ury (1981) and by Raiffa (1982) and Mnookin et al. (2000) on the
  negotiator's dilemma.
- **[the bet](process_dealing_bet.md)** (bridge): the exposure accepted on conduct that cannot be
  controlled, and the cheapest instrument in the relation, since what it supplies must otherwise be
  bought. Anchored by Deutsch (1958), Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995), and Williamson (1985) on
  what safeguarding costs.
- **[the spiral](process_dealing_spiral.md)** (bridge): the loop where each answer is locally
  correct and the sequence is lose-lose. Anchored by Glasl on the staged hardening and by Deutsch &
  Krauss (1960) on threat capability making both parties worse off; the composite's twist and
  keystone, that everything each side does to make itself safe is what makes the agreement
  unreachable, so the strongest position at a table is the one with the least to threaten with.
