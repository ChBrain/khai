# Capability: Reference

## Line of Work

Capability as **composite**: a persona's belief about its own power -- what it holds about
where ability comes from, what it believes it can do here, and what it will do to keep that
belief from being tested. It is not a new faculty but a wiring of four: the **mindset**
engine supplies the theory of ability, fixed or growable; the **locus-of-control** engine
supplies the expectancy of where outcomes are decided, within the self or outside it; the
**self-efficacy** engine supplies the task-specific confidence that theory yields; and the
**self-handicapping** engine supplies the obstacle erected to keep the belief safe from a
clean verdict. This composite adds no fifth engine; it reads what the four make together --
a self-belief that both guides action and defends itself -- and stages that into three
parts, closing on the discovery that the defence is paid for with the attainment it was
meant to protect.

The domain does not model any single force -- the theory of ability is the mindset engine's,
the expectancy of control is locus-of-control's, the task confidence is self-efficacy's, the
pre-emptive excuse is self-handicapping's, and one alone is a position or a process, not the
whole belief. Nor is it the standing character of pursuit -- the motive, the orientation, the
stamina -- which is the striving composite's; nor the crossing from wanting to doing, which is
the volition composite's; nor the persona's regard for its own worth, which is the self-esteem
engine's, read in the self-relation composite. And it does not model competence itself: what a
persona can actually do is not this engine's business, only what it believes about that. It
owns the self-belief about power: the working theory a persona acts on before ability has said
anything, and guards after.

The spine is the perceived-control tradition -- the finding that belief about one's own power
predicts action better than power itself does. Bandura set the centre: self-efficacy as the
belief in one's capability to organize and execute what an attainment requires, built most
durably by mastery experience and by seeing one's like succeed, and determining what is
attempted and how long effort lasts. Rotter gave the generalized frame: the expectancy that
outcomes follow from one's own action or from luck, fate, and powerful others. Dweck gave the
theory of ability itself: fixed or incremental, a lens that turns the same effort into either
a route to growth or evidence of its absence. And Berglas and Jones read the defence and the
composite's turn: self-handicapping, the obstacle erected before an outcome so that failure
cannot indict ability and success is credited double -- a self-belief made unfalsifiable at
the cost of the performance. The three bridges are those readings staged: the theory, the
reach, and the guard.

## Origin

The psychology of perceived control and self-belief, wired as a composite over the mindset,
locus-of-control, self-efficacy, and self-handicapping engines.

| Source                               | Key Work                                                                 | Scope                                                                                                                                                     |
| :----------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Albert Bandura**                   | _Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control_ (1997)                          | Belief in one's capability to execute what an attainment requires, built by mastery, vicarious experience, and persuasion. The root and the reach bridge. |
| **Julian B. Rotter**                 | _"Generalized expectancies for internal versus external control"_ (1966) | The standing expectancy of where outcomes are decided -- one's own action, or luck, fate, and powerful others. The theory bridge.                         |
| **Carol S. Dweck**                   | _Self-Theories_ (1999); _Mindset_ (2006)                                 | Ability held as a fixed amount or an incremental capacity -- the lens that makes effort a route to growth or evidence of its lack. The theory bridge.     |
| **Steven Berglas & Edward E. Jones** | _"Drug choice as a self-handicapping strategy"_ (1978)                   | The obstacle erected before an outcome so failure cannot indict ability and success counts double. The guard bridge, the composite's keystone.            |

## Restrictions

What the composite refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The single engines (the atoms)**: The composite wires the four but owns none. The theory
  of ability -- fixed or growth -- is the mindset engine's; the expectancy of control --
  internal or external -- is locus-of-control's; the task-specific confidence and its sources
  -- mastery, vicarious, persuasion -- is self-efficacy's; the acquired and claimed obstacle
  and the practised handicapper are self-handicapping's. Capability reads only what the four
  make as one belief; a single engine alone is a position or a process, not the whole.
- **Actual competence**: What a persona can in fact do is not modelled here at all. Capability
  is the belief about power, not the power -- the two can be far apart, and the gap between
  them is much of what the composite is for.
- **The character of pursuit (striving)**: The motive that energizes striving, the orientation
  by which a goal is chased, and the stamina that holds it are the striving composite's.
  Capability is what a persona believes it can do; striving is how it goes about wanting.
- **The crossing from wanting to doing (volition)**: The goal deliberated, the if-then plan,
  and the executive control that holds a pursuit are the volition composite's. Capability is
  the belief that decides whether the crossing is attempted at all.
- **The regard for one's own worth (self-esteem / self-relation)**: How a persona values itself
  overall is the self-esteem engine's, read in the self-relation composite. Capability is the
  narrower belief about what it can do, which feeds that regard without being it.
- **The self-belief as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A persona's belief about its power
  made to **stand for** a story -- the handicapping that mirrors a theme of self-sabotage, the
  fixed mindset that mirrors a moral -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's pitch, not
  the force on the persona. Capability delivers the persona's own working belief; the meaning
  read into it is the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A composite root over four engine atoms it declares as dependencies,
with three bridges, the parts of a self's belief about its own power.

- **[capability](process_capability.md)** (the root): a persona's belief about its own power --
  the theory held, the confidence yielded, the belief defended. Anchored by Bandura (perceived
  control and self-efficacy).
- **[the theory](process_capability_theory.md)** (bridge): where ability comes from and where
  control lies -- fixed or growable, internal or external, the frame that decides what any
  attempt could prove. Anchored by Dweck (mindset) and Rotter (locus of control).
- **[the reach](process_capability_reach.md)** (bridge): the task-specific confidence built by
  mastery, vicarious experience, and persuasion -- the belief that decides what is attempted
  and how long effort lasts. Anchored by Bandura (self-efficacy).
- **[the guard](process_capability_guard.md)** (bridge): the obstacle erected before the
  outcome so failure belongs to it and success counts double. Anchored by Berglas & Jones; the
  composite's twist and keystone, that a self can protect its belief in its ability only by
  never letting the ability be cleanly tested.
