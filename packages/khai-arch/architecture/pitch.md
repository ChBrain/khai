A pitch is the key a production is played in: the same fixed events, tuned to one dominant tone. It is not written into the events; a different pitch is a different production of the same tale, so one play may hold two pitches and stage the same plots twice. A pitch attaches by link, and where it attaches sets what it keys: the play casts its pitch in the Company like the rest of the cast, a plot may link its own so the key turns over the arc, and any other typed file linking a pitch tunes that file alone. A play casting one pitch has named its default and owes no more; a play carrying more than one must explain the carry, in the Company, in prose: which is the default, and when the others take the run. And the key is an opinion held twice, both valid: the playwright writes the play's default, the director staging a run may key it otherwise, and a run keyed against the default is a different production, not a broken one. It is the linked twin of the `voice` frontmatter field, and like the voice it never cascades: the pitch keys the scope that links it, the voice tunes the single file carrying it.

- **T**enor: The dominant register the run is tuned to, the key its events are played in.

- **U**ndertow: The counter-current beneath the tenor, the pull against it that keeps the tone from going flat.

- **N**erve: What the pitch presses in the audience, the feeling it is built to strike.

- **E**cho: What the pitch leaves once the run is over, the aftertaste that lingers.

---

Tenor and Nerve are the tone and its target; Undertow and Echo are what keeps it honest and what it leaves behind. A pitch succeeds when the same events, tuned to it, strike a feeling the bare account never could, without changing what happens.
