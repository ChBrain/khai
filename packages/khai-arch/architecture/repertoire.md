The Company is closed. The library is not.

A repertoire is what a production can reach without owning it: the castable
world a house declares once, so that every play inside it may cast members it
did not write. The rule is the same at every scale: a play borrows from its
house as a house borrows from other houses, and engines were the first
lenders.

- **L**ibrary: What is in reach. A house's repertoire is its own collection,
  the houses it declares, and the engines it declares, each shelf describing
  itself through its registry or manifest. Declared once as dependencies, read
  as one union catalog; nothing is loaded until a Company names it.

- **O**wn: What is built. The thin layer a play holds itself: the files
  authored beside the borrowed because no shelf holds them. A production owns
  its occasion, never its world; a play that owns everything has not used the
  library, and a play that owns nothing is not a play.

- **A**nswer: How a name resolves. Every Company link receives exactly one
  verdict: it resolves in the library (a borrow), it resolves only beside the
  play (a build), it resolves nowhere (a failure), or it resolves more than
  once (qualify it by its lender, or fail). Computed, never judged; a resolver
  that guesses has already broken the world.

- **N**ame: What holds it together. A member's name is its identity
  everywhere: links identify, they do not locate. Names are API: a rename is a
  breaking change, a collision is qualified in the composite convention
  (package plus member file), and a bundle may flatten every path because the
  names survive it.

---

Library and Own are the holdings. Answer and Name are the law. A repertoire
succeeds when borrowing is invisible at the point of play: the cast performs
as one company, whichever shelf each member came from, and what a run teaches
travels home to the shelf that lent it.

Engines are the oldest instance of this rule: content declared as a
dependency, resolved from a package, its law switched on for the whole world.
Groups are the in-house instance: a play whose Company casts plays it does not
own. A scenario is only the general case arrived: mostly Library, thinly Own,
packed flat, played anywhere.
