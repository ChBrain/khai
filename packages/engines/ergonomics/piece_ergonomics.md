# Piece: Ergonomics

## Taxonomy

The root of the ergonomics engine. It models the object's physical fit to the human body: how its dimensions, reach, clearance, and force demand accommodate or fail the body that must use it -- the design of things to human measure. It is distinct from usability, which the usability engine owns (how the form communicates its use to the mind, not how it fits the hand); from agency, which the agency engine owns (what conduct the object enforces, not what body it accommodates); and from the body's own states, which the body engine owns on a persona (fatigue, pain, arousal -- the body's condition, not the object's fit to it). A thing can be perfectly legible and still not fit.

## Place

In the object's dimensions, wherever the body meets it -- the handle's girth, the seat's height, the doorway's width, the control's distance. The fit lives at every surface a body touches, reaches, or passes through, and it travels with the object into every hand and every frame that must use it.

## Load Bearing

What the fit holds up is the body's ability to use the object at all without strain or injury. Remove the fit -- size the object to no real body, or to only one -- and the use does not vanish but it costs: the too-small grip cramps the hand, the too-high shelf bars the short, the too-tight clearance traps the large. The object bears the body's use physically; where the fit fails, the body pays the difference in effort, exclusion, or harm, and the failure is in the object's measure, not the body's.

## Apparent

The fit is mostly not seen but felt. A good fit disappears into ease -- the tool that sits right in the hand, the chair the body forgets it is in -- and shows only in the body's comfort. A bad fit shows in the body's fight with the object: the hunched shoulder, the overstretched reach, the knuckle barked on the tight clearance. What is apparent to the eye is a shape; what is borne by the body is a measure that meets it or misses.

## Yearbook

The history of a fit is written in the bodies that used it -- who it suited and who it strained, the injuries it accumulated, the users it quietly excluded because it was never sized for them. Over a life of use the object's measure is judged by the aches and the accommodations it drew: the workstation dated from the repetitive strain it caused, the cockpit from the pilots it was too small or too large to hold. A design is indicted, late, by the bodies it did not fit.
