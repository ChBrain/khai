# Regime: Reference

## Line of Work

Regime as **position**: the standing arrangement under which a persona's polity decides. It is not a
disposition of the persona and not a move they make; it is the board -- already in place when the
first scene opens, setting what political acts exist and what each one costs, and not changeable by
any single persona inside it.

The engine is a **catalogue**, in the manner of the bias engine, and for the same reason. Nobody uses
a taxonomy of political systems by reasoning from first principles in the moment; they use it by
recognising the type they are looking at and reading off what follows. So the value is in the
coverage and in the entry, not in a general theory: forty members, each named type read for what it
**has**, what it **orders**, what it structurally **loses**, and the two ways a polity can carry that
loss.

It routes through **six axes**, and the six are not a redundancy. The classificatory traditions ask
genuinely different questions of the same arrangement and get non-competing answers, so a single
spine would have had to suppress most of what the field knows. **The count** is Aristotle's: rule by
one, few, or many, each in a true form serving the common good and a deviant form serving the rulers,
which is still the sharpest cut anybody has made and whose weakness -- that the pairs cannot be
distinguished from outside -- is itself instructive. **The claim** is Weber's three grounds of
legitimate domination: traditional, charismatic, legal-rational, which sorts by why the command is
obeyed rather than by who issues it. **The constraint** is the modern comparative spine, running from
liberal democracy through electoral democracy and the competitive and hegemonic authoritarianisms to
the closed autocracy, with **totalitarianism kept as a distinct kind rather than an extreme degree**,
following Linz: it is defined by an ideology and a mobilised participation, not merely by more
repression. **The holder** is Geddes' finding that what matters most about an autocracy is which kind
of body holds it -- a person, an army, a party, a family -- because that predicts how it ends better
than how repressive it is. **The architecture** is the constitutional-design literature, Duverger and
Linz and Tsebelis and Lijphart, which cross-cuts every axis above: parliamentary or presidential,
unitary or federal, majoritarian or consensual. **The substitute** collects what rules where none of
the named forms does, including the cases that are the absence of a regime rather than a kind of one.

The engine's **twist** is an information problem, and it belongs to the root because it is a property
of the instrument rather than of any one type. Every regime buys compliance by making some things
unsayable, and the better it succeeds the less its own public speech carries information. Kuran's
preference falsification is the mechanism: where the cost of dissent is high, personas misrepresent
their private preferences, and since everybody is doing it nobody -- including the regime -- can
estimate how much support is real. Wintrobe named the consequence for the ruler: the more effectively
repression works, the less the ruler can tell a loyal official from a frightened one, whether a policy
is working, or how close the collapse is. So **a regime's power over what may be said is inversely
related to its knowledge of what is true**, and Kuran's own case -- that the 1989 revolutions
surprised the regimes, the opposition, and the analysts alike -- is the demonstration. It is not a
failure of a badly run autocracy. It scales with how well the instrument works.

Each type's **Loses** section is where the engine earns its keep, and the sections were written to
name one structural incapacity rather than a list of drawbacks. Liberal democracy loses speed and the
power to bind its successors. Personalist rule loses a safe exit, which is why it usually ends with
the ruler. Totalitarianism loses the capacity to stop, since a regime organised around a total
project cannot declare the project finished. Competitive authoritarianism loses certainty -- it holds
real elections it must not lose, which is a permanently expensive position. Consensus government loses
the ability to decide against anybody. Technocracy loses the standing to lose an argument. These are
the places where a play's pressure ordinarily belongs.

The engine **does not** model political conduct. Agenda control, coalition-building, logrolling,
stalling, veto points and the rest are the politics engine's, and the division is deliberate: regime
is the board, politics is the play on it. The same move costs differently under each type, which is
exactly why they are separate packages -- a persona's courage or caution is not a fact about their
character until it is priced by what they are risking it against.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This is a catalogue of competing classificatory traditions, not
a single measured taxonomy, and it should be read as one. The six axes come from different research
programmes with different purposes, and they were not designed to be combined; putting them on one
root is khai's editorial move, defensible because they genuinely cross-cut, but not something any of
the cited authors did. Within the axes, confidence varies a great deal. Aristotle's six types are a
conceptual scheme two and a half thousand years old, retained because it is clarifying, not because it
was validated. Weber's three grounds are ideal types, which is Weber's own word for them -- no actual
regime is purely any one. The constraint spectrum is the best-evidenced part and is genuinely
measured, by V-Dem, Polity, and Freedom House among others, but **its middle is contested**: whether
competitive authoritarianism, electoral autocracy, and hybrid regime are three categories, one
category with three names, or points on a continuum with no natural cuts is an open argument, and
serious datasets disagree about specific countries every year. Geddes' regime-type findings on
durability and mode of exit replicate well and are the axis with the clearest predictive record.
Lijphart's majoritarian/consensus contrast is a well-supported description; his stronger claims about
consensus democracy's performance are more disputed. The parliamentary-versus-presidential debate --
Linz's argument that presidentialism is prone to breakdown -- has been contested for thirty years, and
the honest summary is that the correlation is real and the causal story is not settled. Several
substitute terms are worse off still: **kleptocracy, ochlocracy, and plutocracy are used as verdicts
far more often than as descriptions**, and the engine keeps them only on the condition stated in their
own files, that the named basis really is what decides. What should not be taken from this engine is
that a polity has one true type, or that placing it settles anything about how it will behave; the
axes are for reading a situation clearly, and the disagreement between them is usually the most
informative thing on the board.

axes are separately sourced; the twist is Kuran's and Wintrobe's.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Political conduct (politics)**: Agenda control, dimension-splitting, scope-shifting,
  coalition-building, logrolling, stalling, and rule-contest, together with the veto point, the broker
  seat, the bloc hold, the mandate claim, and the outsider stand, are the politics engine's. Regime is
  the board and politics is the play on it. A play should hold both: the type sets what a move costs,
  and the move is still the persona's.
- **The bases and faces of power (power, politics)**: Where a persona's ability to move another comes
  from is the power engine's, and the three faces -- open contest, agenda control, and shaped
  preference -- are the politics engine's. Regime uses both and owns neither.
- **Formal order inside an organisation (hierarchy, org)**: A chain of command within a firm, an
  army, or an institution is the hierarchy and org engines'. Regime's subject is the arrangement of a
  polity; a stratocracy is in this engine because the army **is** the state, not because it has ranks.
- **A total environment run on its inmates (total-institution)**: The prison, the asylum, and the
  barracks as environments that administer a whole life are the total-institution engine's.
  Totalitarianism here is a regime type, and the resemblance in the name is not a shared mechanism.
- **A shared resource and its keeping (commons, upkeep)**: How a group governs a common-pool resource
  -- the monitoring, the graduated sanction, the nested rule-making -- is the commons engine's and the
  upkeep composite's. That is self-governance below the level of a regime, and Ostrom's cases are
  deliberately not states.
- **Living under a power (subjection)**: What being ruled does to a persona -- the internalisation,
  the accommodation, the self-management -- is subjection's. Regime says what they are living under,
  not what it makes of them.
- **Belonging to a people (membership, intergroup)**: Nationhood, citizenship as identity, and the
  relations between groups are the membership and intergroup engines'. Regime treats the demos as
  given and reads only the arrangement that decides for it.
- **Staying or speaking under a bad arrangement (loyalty)**: Exit, voice, and loyalty as a persona's
  options when an organisation or a state declines are the loyalty engine's. Regime sets which of the
  three is available and at what price; it does not model the choosing.
- **The standing type of a place's weather (climate)**: The climate engine owns a member also called
  **regime** -- the arid, humid, tropical, temperate, continental, or polar type a place belongs to
  before any weather falls. Same word, different science, and neither engine is a thinning of the
  other: one is a classification of atmospheres and this one is a classification of rule. The stem is
  whitelisted as a homonym rather than resolved, because resolving it would require one of the two
  fields to stop using its own word.
- **The regime as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A polity's type made to **stand for** a theme
  -- the tyranny that mirrors a household -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's pitch. Regime
  states the arrangement; what it is made to mean is the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A position tree: one root, six axis heads, thirty-three named types.

- **[regime](position_regime.md)** (the root): the standing arrangement by which a territory is
  ruled, read on six crossing axes, carrying the twist -- the more completely a regime controls what
  may be said, the less it can learn what is true. Anchored by Kuran (1995) and Wintrobe (1998).
- **[by the count](position_count_regime.md)**: how many rule, and for whom. Anchored by Aristotle.
  [monarchy](position_monarchy.md), [tyranny](position_tyranny.md),
  [aristocracy](position_aristocracy.md), [oligarchy](position_oligarchy.md),
  [polity](position_polity.md), [ochlocracy](position_ochlocracy.md).
- **[by the claim](position_claim_regime.md)**: what makes the command legitimate. Anchored by Weber
  (1922). [traditional rule](position_traditional_rule.md),
  [charismatic rule](position_charismatic_rule.md),
  [legal-rational rule](position_legal_rational_rule.md).
- **[by the constraint](position_constraint_regime.md)**: whether the rulers can be told no, and
  removed. Anchored by Dahl (1971), Linz (1975), and Levitsky & Way (2010).
  [liberal democracy](position_liberal_democracy.md),
  [electoral democracy](position_electoral_democracy.md),
  [competitive authoritarian](position_competitive_authoritarian.md),
  [electoral autocracy](position_electoral_autocracy.md),
  [closed autocracy](position_closed_autocracy.md), [totalitarian](position_totalitarian.md).
- **[by the holder](position_holder_regime.md)**: what kind of body actually holds an autocracy, and
  therefore how it ends. Anchored by Geddes (1999) and Geddes, Wright & Frantz (2014).
  [personalist](position_personalist.md), [military junta](position_military_junta.md),
  [single party](position_single_party.md), [dynastic](position_dynastic.md).
- **[by the architecture](position_architecture_regime.md)**: how the offices are wired to each
  other, cross-cutting every axis above. Anchored by Linz (1990), Duverger (1980), Tsebelis (2002),
  and Lijphart (1999). [parliamentary](position_parliamentary.md),
  [presidential](position_presidential.md), [semi-presidential](position_semi_presidential.md),
  [unitary](position_unitary.md), [federal](position_federal.md),
  [majoritarian](position_majoritarian.md), [consensus](position_consensus_regime.md).
- **[by the substitute](position_substitute_regime.md)**: what rules where none of the named forms
  does, including the absence of a regime. [theocracy](position_theocracy.md),
  [technocracy](position_technocracy.md), [plutocracy](position_plutocracy.md),
  [kleptocracy](position_kleptocracy.md), [stratocracy](position_stratocracy.md),
  [anarchy](position_anarchy.md), [interregnum](position_interregnum.md).
