# References

## Line of Work

The engine models the blunted capacity for pleasure or interest (anhedonia). Historically treated as a monolithic symptom of depression, modern affective neuroscience dissociates it into distinct components: the motivation to pursue a reward ("wanting" or anticipatory anhedonia) versus the enjoyment of the reward upon acquisition ("liking" or consummatory anhedonia). A persona may lack the drive to initiate an activity despite retaining the capacity to enjoy it, or they may pursue activities habitually only to find no pleasure in them. Furthermore, the deficit can be globally blunted or highly specific, as seen in social anhedonia, where the deficit is isolated to interpersonal connection.

The twist that makes this an engine rather than a definition is the dissociation itself: because the persona may continue to go through the motions (wanting without liking), or because the capacity failure looks like laziness (apathy and blunting), the condition is often misread by others as a mood, an attitude, or a character flaw, rather than a broken capacity.

**Evidence limits**: The boundary between a capacity failure (anhedonia) and a valuation choice (reward/appraisal) is phenomenologically fragile. A persona who stops doing something because they judge it not worth the effort is operating the `reward` or `appraisal` engines, not anhedonia. Anhedonia requires the _capacity_ for the interest or pleasure to be the broken mechanism.

## Restrictions

- **Reward**: The discounting of delayed rewards, the calibration of effort-to-payoff, and the preference for immediate stimulation belong to `reward`. Anhedonia is a capacity deficit, not a discount rate.
- **Joy**: The appraisal of an event as inherently positive and the expression of that affect belong to `joy`. Anhedonia is the failure of the mechanism that makes joy possible.
- **Interest**: The focused attention on a novel or complex object belongs to `interest`. Anhedonia is the blunting of that capacity.
- **Motivation**: The general drive to act and the mobilization of resources belong to `motivation`. Anticipatory anhedonia specifically blunts the reward-seeking aspect of motivation, but non-reward-based motivation (e.g., duty, fear) belongs elsewhere.
- **Diagnosis**: This is authored persona-architecture for a fictional play. It is never a clinical assessment or diagnosis of a real person.
