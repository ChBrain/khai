# Belief in a Just World: Reference

## Line of Work

Belief in a just world as a **position** engine: a persona's standing conviction that the world is fair -- that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get -- taken across the two audiences the belief holds for. The domain does not model the specific attribution move by which an innocent victim is searched for the flaw that explains their fate, which is owned as the bias engine's just-world-hypothesis member; nor the pleasure at a deserved misfortune, which is the schadenfreude engine's; nor the neutral evaluation of one's standing against others, which is the comparison engine's; nor the fairness intuition applied to a particular act, which is the moral-judgment engine's. It models the standing worldview itself and its two validated faces: the personal belief that buffers the self, and the general belief that judges the other.

The spine is Lerner's just-world theory. Lerner proposed that people need to believe the world is a just place where individuals get what they deserve, because that belief lets them commit to long-term goals and trust that effort will be repaid -- and that when the belief is threatened by the suffering of an innocent victim, and justice cannot be restored by helping them, people restore it cognitively instead, by derogating or blaming the victim so that the suffering becomes deserved. Hafer and Begue's review consolidates the evidence: the belief functions as an adaptive resource and a driver of victim derogation, and matters most where the threat to it is real and the injustice cannot be undone. Dalbert's central refinement is that the belief is not one thing but two separable convictions -- a personal belief that the world is fair to oneself, which is the stronger buffer for wellbeing, mental health, and the capacity to invest in the future, and a general belief that the world is fair to people at large, which is more closely tied to harsh social attitudes, endorsement of the status quo, and derogation of the disadvantaged. The altitudes are those: the standing just-world conviction, and its personal and general faces.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The just-world attribution bias (bias)**: The engine models the standing belief, not the specific attribution move it can drive. The just-world-hypothesis member of the bias engine owns the cognitive tilt by which an innocent sufferer is re-tried and found wanting -- the victim searched for the carelessness, character, or choice that explains their fate; belief in a just world is the standing worldview of which that derogation is one defense, and it also holds the personal, self-buffering face the bias member does not touch. The bias is the attribution; this engine is the conviction beneath it, and its adaptive side.
- **Schadenfreude**: The engine models the belief in desert, not the pleasure at a deserved misfortune. Schadenfreude -- the enjoyment of another's setback, sharpened when the setback is judged deserved -- is owned by the schadenfreude engine; belief in a just world can supply the deservingness judgment that schadenfreude feeds on, but the belief is the standing worldview and the pleasure is the emotion. The one holds the world fair; the other enjoys the fair-seeming fall.
- **Comparison**: The engine models the assumption of desert, not the neutral evaluation of standing. Social comparison -- the sizing of oneself against others on some dimension -- is owned by the comparison engine; belief in a just world adds the moral overlay that the resulting differences are deserved, which comparison itself does not assert. Comparison ranks; the just-world belief moralizes the ranking.
- **Moral judgment**: The engine models the standing worldview, not the fairness verdict on a particular act. The intuition of fairness or unfairness applied to a specific action -- whether this was just -- is the moral-judgment engine's; belief in a just world is the prior, global assumption that the world as a whole is fair, which colours but is not identical to any single fairness judgment. The one is a worldview; the other a verdict.
