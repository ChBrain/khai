# Piece: Signifier

## Taxonomy

[Usability](piece_usability.md), the facet this is a part of. Signifier models the perceivable cue that tells the user where and how to act -- the "push" plate, the arrow, the highlighted button, the wear-mark that shows where other hands have gone. Norman introduced the signifier to repair the confusion in perceived affordance: the affordance is the possibility, the signifier is the sign that announces it. It is distinct from grip (the action the form offers, cue or no cue) and from feedback (which answers the action after): the signifier speaks before the act, saying here, this way.

## Place

On the object's surface, at the point of action -- the label on the plate, the marking by the control, the glow on the live button, the path worn to the right door. The signifier sits exactly where the action is to be taken, or it fails: a sign in the wrong place misdirects as surely as no sign at all.

## Load Bearing

What the signifier holds up is the user finding the action at all. An affordance no one can perceive is, for use, an affordance that is not there: the hidden control, the flush panel with no seam, the gesture no cue reveals. Remove the signifier and the object may still afford the action, but the user cannot tell, and an undiscoverable use is a use that does not happen. The signifier bears discoverability -- the difference between a thing that can be operated and one that is.

## Apparent

The cue is what is plainly added to the form to speak for it: the word, the icon, the color, the light, the arrow, the shaped recess that says "finger here." A good signifier is unambiguous and where the action is; a bad one is a signifier that lies (the "pull" on a door that pushes) or clutters (a panel of identical unlabeled buttons, every one a guess). What the object shows about where to act, over and above what its bare form affords, is the signifier.

## Yearbook

The history of a signifier is whether people read it and acted right. A cue that worked disappears into smooth use; one that misled leaves a trail of wrong actions -- the button everyone presses first and shouldn't, the door with the smudge on the wrong side. Conventions accrete here too: the red that came to mean stop, the underline that came to mean link, learned over a long collective history until the sign reads as natural. What a signifier means is settled by the record of how it was actually read.
