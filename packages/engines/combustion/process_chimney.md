# Process: Chimney Effect

## Taxonomy

[Combustion](process_combustion.md), the phenomenon of fire drawn up a vertical or inclined shaft.

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: combustion

## Initiated by

Hot buoyant gases in a vertical or inclined channel drive a strong draught that pulls fire and smoke upward.
Chimney effect -- the stack effect, and on an incline the trench effect -- starts wherever a temperature-over-height pressure difference forms in a stairwell, lift shaft, duct, or street canyon; the induced draught feeds fire whatever lines the channel, and on an inclined surface the flame attaches (a Coanda effect) and preheats the fuel above it.
It is fire given a flue: a channel that turns a local fire into a fast vertical run.

## Direction

Up the shaft, and up the slope, faster than crews can follow.
Chimney effect drives fire and smoke rocketing up shafts and pulls fresh air in below; on an incline the trench effect (as at King's Cross, 1987) causes a sudden eruptive flashover up the slope. High-rise stack effect can even reverse seasonally with outdoor temperature.
The direction is vertical acceleration: heat and smoke building at levels far above the seat, ahead of any hand.

## Lever

The induced draught against the door that, opened, becomes the flue.
The carrying force is the buoyancy-driven pressure difference over height; the resistance fails because opening a stairwell or shaft door can turn it into a chimney, drawing fire upward through the building, and any breach reshuffles the whole flow network. Pressurisation and door control are decisive.
Seal and pressurise the shaft and the draught is checked; open it and the building becomes the fire's chimney.

## Echo

The upper floors filling with heat and smoke while the seat is far below.
Chimney effect is read by its signature -- a roaring vertical draught, smoke rocketing up shafts, rapid heat build at upper levels far from the fire, an audible rush in stairwells. It hands smoke and heat to the building's top and, on an incline, an eruptive run up the slope.
Chimney effect is dated from the draught, the moment the shaft's buoyancy turned a fire into a vertical run.
