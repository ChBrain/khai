# Process: Chaparral Fire

## Taxonomy

[Combustion](process_combustion.md), the wildland phenomenon of the volatile-oil brush fire.

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: combustion

## Initiated by

Dense drought-stressed shrubland, oily and waxy and half-dead, ignites and burns explosively.
Chaparral fire starts in sclerophyllous shrubs -- chamise, ceanothus, manzanita, and the Mediterranean maquis and garrigue analogues -- laden with volatile oils and waxes and heavy dead-fuel fractions; it burns intense and hot, roughly 1000-1100 C, with long flame lengths.
It is the brush fire at its most violent: fuel that does not merely burn but volatilises, throwing flame and igniting from radiant heat well ahead of the front.

## Direction

Wind-driven and leaping, the volatile foliage carrying fire ahead of itself.
Chaparral fire runs hardest under offshore downslope wind events -- the Santa Ana, the Diablo -- when low live-fuel moisture, wind, and slope align; old decadent stands carry so much dead fuel they burn as a near-continuous canopy, and prolific spotting jumps every line.
The direction is a high-intensity wind-driven run in which the fuel ignites ahead of the flame by radiation alone, so the fire is already across the gap the line was meant to hold.

## Lever

The volatile-oil chemistry against the containment that the radiant heat has already crossed.
The carrying force is foliage that gives off flammable volatiles and preheats the fuel ahead by radiation; the resistance fails because high intensity and long flame length forbid direct attack, wind-driven spotting jumps firelines, and the fuel lights from radiant heat before the front arrives -- so water and retardant lines are outflanked and suppression typically fails until the wind drops or the fuel changes.
Meet it in a wind event and nothing holds; wait for the wind to fail or the fuel to thin and only then can it be stopped.

## Echo

The hillside burned to bare soil, hot and total, the fire adapted to and needing.
Chaparral fire leaves a high-severity burn and is read by its signature -- tall deep orange-red flame, dark oily soot-rich smoke, strong resinous-aromatic smell, a loud roar with popping and venting of volatiles. It hands its ground to a fire-adapted regeneration that its own seedbank is built to meet.
Chaparral fire is dated from the ignition that became a blow-up, the moment the volatile brush stopped smouldering and detonated into a run.
