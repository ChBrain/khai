# Hatred: Reference

## Line of Work

Hatred as a **process** engine: a durable, eliminatory sentiment toward a devalued other -- the standing wish to distance from, harm, and diminish a target seen as bad to the core -- taken across the components the sentiment runs on. The domain does not model the incident-level reaction to a specific provocation, which is the anger engine's; nor the cold, disengaged looking-down that dismisses, which is the contempt engine's; nor the visceral revulsion reflex, which is the disgust engine's; nor the behavioral infliction of harm, which is the aggression engine's; nor the moral licensing of that harm, which is the moral-disengagement engine's. It models the standing sentiment of hate itself: sustained, aimed at elimination, and anchored in a verdict on the target's essence.

The spine is the psychology of hate. Sternberg's duplex theory of hate casts it, in parallel with his triangular theory of love, as compounded of three components: the negation of intimacy (a distancing and revulsion, the repudiation of closeness), passion (an intense anger or fear, the active hostility), and decision/commitment (the devaluation and diminution of the target through contempt, and the cognitive commitment to its badness), whose combinations yield the varieties of hate. Fischer and colleagues' review frames hate as an emotional attitude or sentiment rather than a brief emotion: more stable and enduring than anger, aimed at the target's elimination or at least its harm, and grounded in a stable, dispositional attribution -- the target is seen as malevolent by its very nature, so a single act is generalized into a verdict on the person and, often, the group. This dispositional, essentializing appraisal is what makes hate durable where anger passes, and what lets it generalize from a person to a category. The altitudes are those: the hating sentiment, its repulsion, its malice, and its essentializing.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Anger**: The engine models the durable sentiment, not the incident emotion. Anger -- the reaction to a specific provocation or goal-blockage, hot and time-limited, aimed at redress -- is owned by the anger engine; hatred is the standing orientation that outlasts any single provocation and aims at the target's elimination rather than its correction. Anger can feed hatred, but the flare passes where the sentiment holds. The one is an episode; the other is a settled stance.
- **Contempt**: The engine models the engaged, eliminatory sentiment, not the disengaged dismissal. Contempt -- the cold looking-down that judges the other beneath regard and turns away -- is owned by the contempt engine; hatred is hotter and more invested, wanting the target's undoing rather than merely dismissing it. Contempt's devaluation feeds hate's essentializing, but contempt disengages where hate leans in. The two differ in heat and aim: dismissal versus elimination.
- **Disgust**: The engine models the interpersonal negation of intimacy, not the visceral revulsion reflex. Disgust -- the reflexive recoil from the contaminating -- is owned by the disgust engine; hatred's repulsion recruits that recoil but is the deliberate refusal of closeness to a devalued person, an interpersonal distancing rather than a reflex. Disgust supplies the felt revulsion; hatred directs it as the negation of a tie.
- **Aggression**: The engine models the sentiment, not the harmful act. Aggression -- the behavioral infliction of harm, and the pleasure at an enemy's deserved misfortune -- is owned by the aggression and schadenfreude engines; hatred is the standing wish that may motivate harm but is not the harming itself. The malice is the desire; the aggression is the deed.
- **Moral disengagement**: The engine models the essentializing appraisal, not the licensing of harm. Moral disengagement -- the dehumanizing and self-exculpating moves that switch off self-sanction so harm can be done without guilt -- is owned by the moral-disengagement engine; hatred's essentializing fixes the target's badness as essence, which can feed dehumanization but is the sentiment's own appraisal, not the mechanism that frees the persona to act on it. The one condemns the enemy's nature; the other clears the persona's conscience.
