# Position: By the Making

## Taxonomy

[Law](position_law.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: law

## Has

Hart's answer to what a legal system is made of: not one kind of rule but two.
Primary rules tell personas what to do and not do. Secondary rules are rules **about** the primary ones -- how to tell which count, how to change them, how to settle whether one was broken -- and it is their presence that separates a legal order from a set of customs strongly held.
A regime of primary rules alone is possible, and small enough societies run on one, but it is static, uncertain, and inefficient in exactly the three ways the three secondary rules answer.

## Orders

Trace the rule upward before arguing about it.
Every genuine legal question is really a question about which secondary rule is in play: whether this counts as law at all, whether it was validly changed, or whether this body is the one that gets to say. A dispute that looks like a disagreement about right and wrong is very often a disagreement about recognition.

## Loses

Any ground outside itself.
The rule of recognition cannot be validated by a further rule without beginning an infinite regress, so at the bottom of every legal order is a practice rather than a proof -- officials converging on treating certain things as law, for no reason the system itself can supply.
Which means a legal order's foundation is a social fact about what officials do, and it holds exactly as long as they keep doing it.

## Drives

On the loss of ground outside itself:
admits that the foundation is a convergent practice and works to keep officials converged, since that practice is the only thing holding,
or claims a warrant above the law -- nature, scripture, the people, the founding -- and gains a foundation it can point at, at the cost of handing every challenger the same move.
