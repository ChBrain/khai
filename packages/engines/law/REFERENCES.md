# Law: Reference

## Line of Work

Law as **position**: legality as the standing condition a persona's conduct is judged under. Not a
disposition and not a move -- a rule system that claims to bind them, which they did not consent to
and cannot individually revise.

The engine's first job is a distinction the other norm engines do not make. A **norm** binds because
it is shared; a **command** binds because somebody with force behind them issued it; a **law** binds
because **another rule says it does**. That second rule is the whole difference, and it is why the
tightness of a society's norms, the sanction a peer will pay to impose one, and the obedience a
persona gives an authority are all separate phenomena owned elsewhere. Law is what happens when a
rule about rules exists.

Four axes, each a different question. **The making** is Hart's: a legal system is the union of
primary rules of obligation with secondary rules about them -- recognition, change, adjudication --
which cure respectively the uncertainty, the static, and the inefficiency of a regime of primary rules
alone. Its consequence is the one that matters most: **the rule of recognition cannot itself be
validated**, because there is nothing above it to do the validating, so the foundation of any legal
order is a convergent practice among officials rather than a proof, and it holds as long as they keep
converging.

**The failing** is Fuller's eight, and the axis is built as a catalogue of failures on purpose. These
are not eight kinds of bad law but eight kinds of not-law: no rules at all, unpublished, retroactive,
unintelligible, contradictory, impossible, unstable, and administered otherwise than declared. The
test that unifies them is procedural rather than moral -- could a persona of goodwill comply? -- and
several of them turn out to converge on the same downstream condition. An impossible rule and a
contradictory rule both produce universal breach, which is selective enforcement seen from the other
end; an unintelligible rule transfers its content to whoever applies it, which is discretion arrived
at by accident. The eighth, administered otherwise, swallows the other seven: a technically defective
rule administered sensibly can still govern, and a perfect rule administered otherwise cannot.

**The standing** is Ewick and Silbey's, and it is the persona-facing axis. Ordinary people do not
hold one attitude toward law; they occupy one of three standings and move between them within a
single week. **Before the law** it is majestic, impartial, remote -- respected, and not approached.
**With the law** it is a game with rules and players, to be used by whoever knows how. **Against the
law** it is an arbitrary power to be evaded, joked about, and worked around. The three are
contradictory and held simultaneously without discomfort, and that is not a public misunderstanding to
be corrected: it is how legality is actually inhabited, and it is what makes it durable. A system
nobody wholly believes in is also one nobody is wholly disappointed by.

**The gap** is Pound's, with the tradition that grew from it. Law in books and law in action are
different objects and only one reaches a persona, and the gap is produced by ordinary features of any
working order rather than by corruption: Lipsky's street-level bureaucrat, whose coping routines under
an impossible caseload **are** the policy; selective enforcement, which tracks visibility and
vulnerability because those are what enforcement is cheap against; the dead letter, kept because
repealing it would require somebody to argue publicly for the conduct it forbids; Galanter's repeat
player, who litigates the good facts and settles the bad ones and thereby selects the sample that
becomes precedent, without a single wrong decision being made; and Merry's legal pluralism, more than
one normative order on the same ground, of which Ellickson's cattle ranchers are the sharp case --
neighbours settling substantial disputes by norms none of them could state, with the state law that
formally governs sitting unused a few miles away. The axis's own loss follows: because the gap is made
by the conditions of application rather than by the words, **amending the text moves the gap rather
than closing it**, which is why legal reform so reliably disappoints its drafters and so reliably
helps the parties who were already doing well.

The **twist** is at the root, because it belongs to the instrument rather than to any one form. A law
is enforceable only if the consequence of breaking it is determinate, and a determinate consequence is
a tariff -- so **no law can forbid without also pricing**. Gneezy and Rustichini's daycare study is
the demonstration and it is worse than a paradox: introducing a fine for late collection made lateness
**more** common, because an obligation to the staff had been converted into a transaction with the
institution, and when the fine was withdrawn the lateness stayed high, because the obligation did not
convert back. The conversion runs one way. A legal order can turn a wrong into a price at any time,
cannot turn a price back into a wrong, and cannot enforce anything at all without doing some of the
converting.

The engine **does not** model who rules. That is the regime engine's, and the division is the same one
politics and regime already hold: regime is the arrangement that decides, law is what the deciding
produces and how it reaches a persona. Nor does it model why a persona obeys, which is obedience's,
nor the norm-making power that trains conduct without any rule being stated, which is discipline's.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This is a jurisprudential and socio-legal engine, and the two
halves rest on very different kinds of support. The making and the failing axes are **conceptual
frameworks, not findings**. Hart's two-level account of a legal system is the most influential in
modern analytic jurisprudence and is still argued with -- Dworkin's objection, that hard cases are
settled by principles the rule of recognition cannot identify, is the standard one and has not been
disposed of. Fuller's eight are a normative argument that his own critics, Hart among them, read as
merely instrumental: an efficient tyranny can satisfy all eight and rule wickedly, and the engine says
so in the axis head rather than burying it, because the constraint the eight buy is real but narrow.
The standing and gap axes are **empirical and better supported**, though as qualitative and
observational work rather than as experiment. Ewick and Silbey's three standings come from a large
interview study and have been widely replicated in form, but the typology is interpretive and its
boundaries are argued about. Galanter's repeat-player thesis has held up across four decades and many
jurisdictions and is about as well established as anything in the field. Lipsky's account is
ethnographic and has been extended rather than tested. The **weakest single claim** is the twist's
generality: the daycare result is a real, well-replicated experiment showing that a sanction can crowd
out a moral obligation asymmetrically, and the crowding-out literature around it is substantial -- but
the daycare setting is small, relational, and repeated, and the leap from it to legal sanction in
general is an inference the engine makes and should be read as making. Deterrence works in plenty of
domains, and Tyler's finding that compliance tracks perceived procedural fairness far more than
sanction severity is a live, well-evidenced alternative account of why people obey that this engine
delegates rather than refutes. What should not be taken from the engine is that pricing always
backfires, or that a legal order's gap is a scandal rather than a structural feature. The claim is
narrower: expect any determinate sanction to teach some of the tariff along with the prohibition, and
expect a persona's real experience of law to be made at the counter rather than in the statute.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Who rules, and how (regime, politics)**: The arrangement by which a polity decides is the regime
  engine's, and the conduct of deciding is politics'. Regime is the arrangement, law is what the
  deciding produces and how it reaches a persona. A play should hold all three: the regime sets what
  law can be made, politics is the making, and this engine is the rule once it is out and pointing at
  somebody.
- **Why a persona obeys (obedience)**: Compliance with a legitimate authority against one's own
  conscience is the obedience engine's, and Tyler's procedural-justice account of why people obey
  belongs with it. Law says what a persona is subject to, not what makes them comply.
- **The norm-making power that needs no rule (discipline)**: Foucault's continuous, impersonal
  training by watching and measuring, until a persona holds themselves to the norm unwatched, is the
  discipline engine's. It does its work without any rule being stated, which is precisely the
  difference from a legal order.
- **How strict a society's norms are (tightness-looseness)**: The strength of norms and the latitude
  granted for deviance are Gelfand's and the tightness-looseness engine's. That is a property of a
  culture; this engine's subject is a rule that binds because another rule says so.
- **The costly sanctioning of a defector (altruistic-punishment)**: A persona paying out of their own
  pocket to make a norm-breaker answer, with nothing coming back, is the altruistic-punishment
  engine's. That is peer enforcement without an office, and no rule of adjudication is involved.
- **What one persona holds another to have coming (deserving, condemnation, moral-account)**: Moral
  judgment, desert, the CAD triad, and the ledgers of guilt, repair, and forgiveness are those
  composites'. Law is indifferent to whether the wronged party feels satisfied, which is exactly what
  makes it a separate thing.
- **The record's authority (writ, document, speech-act)**: How a saying changes the world, how
  inscription fixes it, and why a room acts on a record it cannot check are the speech-act and document
  engines' and the writ composite's. Law uses all three -- a statute is a writ that worked -- and owns
  none of them.
- **The person made by institutional power (subjection)**: What being governed does to a persona --
  the forming, the training, the surrender -- is the subjection composite's. Law states the condition;
  what it makes of the persona living in it is subjection's.
- **An obligation between two persons (owing)**: The gift, the debt, and the settling are the owing
  composite's. An obligation becomes this engine's only once a rule about rules stands behind it.
- **Legality as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A trial or a statute made to **stand for** a
  theme -- the unreadable rule that mirrors a motif of confusion -- is refused: that is authored key,
  the play's pitch. Law states the condition; the meaning read into it is the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A position tree: one root, four axis heads, twenty named forms.

- **[law](position_law.md)** (the root): a rule system that claims to bind, binding because another
  rule says it does, carrying the twist -- no law can forbid without also pricing, and the conversion
  runs one way. Anchored by Hart (1961) and Gneezy & Rustichini (2000).
- **[by the making](position_making_law.md)**: what turns a rule into law and keeps it one. Anchored
  by Hart (1961). [the primary rule](position_primary_rule.md),

  [the rule of change](position_rule_of_change.md),
  [the rule of adjudication](position_rule_of_adjudication.md).
- **[by the failing](position_failing_law.md)**: the eight ways a rule system stops being law without
  anybody repealing anything. Anchored by Fuller (1964). [no rules at all](position_no_rules.md),
  [the unpublished rule](position_unpublished_rule.md),
  [the retroactive rule](position_retroactive_rule.md),
  [the unintelligible rule](position_unintelligible_rule.md),
  [the contradictory rule](position_contradictory_rule.md),
  [the impossible rule](position_impossible_rule.md), [the unstable rule](position_unstable_rule.md),
  [administered otherwise](position_administered_otherwise.md).
- **[by the standing](position_standing_law.md)**: the stance a persona takes toward legality, held
  contradictorily and simultaneously. Anchored by Ewick & Silbey (1998).
  [before the law](position_before_the_law.md), [with the law](position_with_the_law.md),

- **[by the gap](position_gap_law.md)**: the distance between the rule declared and the rule as it

  [selective enforcement](position_selective_enforcement.md), [the dead letter](position_dead_letter.md),
  [the repeat player](position_repeat_player.md), [legal pluralism](position_legal_pluralism.md).
