# Structural Model: Reference

## Line of Work

The structural model as a **position** engine: Freud's second topography of the psyche -- three agencies, the id, the ego, and the superego, in standing dynamic conflict over a drive, a prohibition, and the demands of reality -- taken across the three agencies. The domain does not model the conscious self-concept, which is the identity engine's; nor the ego's specific in-the-moment override of an impulse, which is the self-control engine's; nor its unconscious defensive manoeuvres, which are the defense engine's; nor the standing moral trait the superego internalizes, which is the moral-identity engine's; nor the reward value of what the id craves, which is the reward engine's. It models the whole three-agency structure and the conflict among the agencies -- the metapsychological architecture beneath a persona's inner life.

The spine is Freud's structural theory. Having first divided the mind topographically into conscious, preconscious, and unconscious systems, Freud recast it in The Ego and the Id into three functional agencies. The id is the oldest, the reservoir of the instinctual drives -- the libidinal and, in the later dual-drive theory, the aggressive -- ruled by the pleasure principle: wholly unconscious, timeless, heedless of logic and contradiction, pressing for the immediate discharge of tension. The superego is the internalized moral authority, heir to the parental prohibitions and to the resolution of the Oedipus complex: a conscience that punishes transgression, and the very wish of it, with guilt, and an ego-ideal that sets the standard the persona must reach. The ego is the executive, ruled by the reality principle: the mediator that stands between the id's demands, the superego's prohibitions, and the external world, deferring, redirecting, and defending, and feeling anxiety as the signal of a conflict it cannot contain. The model is metapsychological -- a theory of the mind's architecture rather than a single empirical finding -- and remains among the most influential frameworks in the history of psychology.

The structural model is not solo work: it founded a tradition that developed and grounded it beyond Freud. Ego psychology took up the ego above all -- Anna Freud's turn to the ego as an agency worth study in its own right (_The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence_), and Heinz Hartmann's account of the ego's autonomous, conflict-free functions and its work of adaptation, which grew the ego from the id's servant into a structure with its own capacities and development. And contemporary neuropsychoanalysis (Mark Solms) works to ground the agencies and their drives in the brain, mapping the id's instinctual pressure onto the subcortical affective systems and testing the metapsychology against neuroscience rather than leaving it a purely clinical theory. The engine takes the tripartite structure as its anchor and the three agencies as its expressions, and reads them as this lineage left them. The altitudes are those: the structural model, and its id, ego, and superego agencies.

## Origin

Freud's structural theory of the psyche and the tradition that developed it -- ego psychology and neuropsychoanalysis -- applied directly as engine constraints across position. Freud gave the spine; the builders after him grew the ego and grounded the model in the brain.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The conscious self-concept (identity)**: The engine models the metapsychological architecture, not the story a persona tells itself about who it is. The conscious, narratable self -- its commitments, its continuity, its felt identity -- is owned by the identity engine; the structural model is the deeper three-agency structure that self rests on and is largely unaware of. Identity is the self as experienced; the structural model is the psyche as theorized beneath it.
- **The ego's impulse-override (self-control)**: The engine models the ego as the whole mediating executive, not its specific act of overriding a temptation. The in-the-moment exertion of willpower against an impulse is owned by the self-control engine; that override is one function of the ego, which also defers, redirects, defends, and reckons with reality far beyond any single act of restraint. Self-control is one thing the ego does; the ego is the agency that does it.
- **The ego's defenses (defense)**: The engine models the ego as mediator, not the taxonomy of its unconscious defensive manoeuvres. The anxiety-driven distortions by which the ego wards off what it cannot bear -- ranked from mature to immature -- are owned by the defense engine; the structural model's ego is the agency that deploys them, delegating their catalogue to defense. The ego defends; how it defends is the defense engine's.
- **The superego's moral trait and verdicts (moral-identity, moral-judgment)**: The engine models the superego as an intrapsychic agency, not the conscious moral self or its judgments. The centrality of being moral to the self-concept is the moral-identity engine's, and the verdict on a particular act is the moral-judgment engine's; the superego is the internalized authority that generates guilt and holds the ego-ideal, of which those are the conscious and evaluative faces. The superego is the judge within; moral-identity and moral-judgment are the self that owns and applies its rulings.
- **The id's reward and affect (reward, emotion)**: The engine models the id as the standing reservoir of drive-pressure, not the valuation or the feeling. The reward value of what the id craves is the reward engine's, and the felt emotions its drives become are the emotion engine's; the id is the pressure beneath them, not the appraisal or the affect itself.
