# Process: The Reckoning

## Taxonomy

[Bidding](process_bidding.md), what the winner finds out, and where the engine is seen for one thing.

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: bidding

## Initiated by

The thing turns out to be worth what it is worth.
The Reckoning starts once the object stops being a prospect: [the overpay](process_the_overpay.md) discovered late and partially, [the defence](process_the_defence.md) mounted because the price is now the winner's judgement, and [the next contest](process_the_next_contest.md), entered on the same terms.

## Direction

Toward a loss that is difficult to see and impossible to publish.
The overpayment does not arrive as a number. It arrives as a project that runs long, a hire who is fine rather than exceptional, a property that does not appreciate, a contract that consumes more than it returns -- each of which has a dozen explanations, and only one of them is the price.
So the evidence for the curse is available to almost nobody who is subject to it.

## Lever

The reality of the overpayment against the impossibility of attributing it.
The carrying force is that the alternative explanations are usually true as well: the project did run long for its own reasons, the market did turn, the hire is doing well enough, and a winner who blamed every subsequent difficulty on the purchase price would be reasoning badly; the resistance is that this makes the price the one factor that never gets charged, since it is fixed, historical, and shared by nothing else -- so the single common cause is the single cause that is never assigned.
The Reckoning rides the engine's keystone knife-edge: winning is the evidence that you were wrong.

## Echo

An outcome that is disappointing for reasons nobody traces to the bid.
It shows the three earlier movements complete -- a contest that supplied appetite, an estimating whose spread nobody saw, a mechanism that took the maximum -- and adds the reason it will happen again, which is that the loss arrives disguised as something else.
This is why the Reckoning is the hardest of the four to stage and the only one worth closing on: the winner must have been competent, the process must have been fair, the price must have been defensible on the day, and the audience must be able to see that the thing which went wrong is the thing that was celebrated.
A play that stages the winner as reckless has replaced the mechanism with a character, and one that stages the seller as sharp has replaced it with a swindle.
The Reckoning is dated years after the award and is never entered against it.
