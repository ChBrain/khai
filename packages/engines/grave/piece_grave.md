# Piece: Grave

## Taxonomy

The marked place of the dead (no khai file of its own yet).

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: grave

## Place

In ground the family does not own, at a distance from where the family lives. Since the nineteenth century the dead have been moved out of the parish yard and onto managed land at the edge of the settlement, held by a council, a company, or a trust.
What a household usually buys is not the earth but a right of burial for a term, so this is the only property most people hold that they pay for, visit, maintain, and can neither occupy nor sell.
Its location is chosen in the worst week of somebody's life, under time pressure, and cannot afterwards be revised.

## Load Bearing

What it holds up is that this person belonged to somebody. A body in unmarked ground has been disposed of; a body under a name has been kept, and every society that can afford to do the second one does it.
It also holds up the claim of the people still alive: that they are a family with a line, that they came from somewhere, and that they are the kind of people who look after their own.
Remove it and the person survives in memory and in records -- both portable, and neither of which can be visited. What goes is not the remembering. What goes is the address.

## Apparent

Read as the dead person's place and built as an institution of the living. The dead have no use for it, cannot see it, and did not choose most of what is on it.
What shows is a name, two dates, and a line of text selected by relatives in a fortnight and priced by the letter, next to a container of flowers in one of three conditions.
What actually shows is how the survivors are doing. Passers-by read the plot before they read the stone, and what they read from it is not about the person under it.

## Yearbook

The arc runs from the burial to the weekly visit to the anniversary to the year nobody comes. Attendance falls off a curve that no family has ever agreed to, and the object outlasts the attention by decades -- often by longer than the term that was paid for.
The engine's twist is here. **A grave is the only part of a death anybody can still maintain, which is why it is the part the living are judged on.** Nothing else about a death can be tended. The loss cannot be worked on, the person cannot be helped, and the feeling cannot be shown to anybody at a distance -- but the plot can be weeded, and the weeding is visible. So the one available task becomes the measure, and a family that is grieving hard and living two hundred miles away keeps a worse grave than a family that is not grieving much and lives nearby.
It is not the internal bond a mourner carries forward, which is grief's, not the rite that puts the body in the ground, which is ritual's, not the object withheld from exchange to carry a lineage, which is heirloom's, and not the record an institution holds of a person, which is document's; and a grave made to **stand for** a theme is authored key, the play's pitch.
It fails by outliving its lease, by outliving the people who knew the name, and by being read as a verdict on whoever is left.
