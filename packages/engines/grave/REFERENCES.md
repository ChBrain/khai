# Grave: Reference

## Line of Work

Grave as **piece**: the marked place of the dead, held on a term by the living, and read by anybody who
walks past.

The engine's subject is an object that does none of its work for the person under it. Laqueur's history
is the spine, and its argument is the operative one: the dead have no use for what is done with their
remains, and every society that can afford to does it anyway, at enormous cost, because the work is for
the living and it is foundational rather than sentimental. Once that is taken seriously the grave stops
being a memorial and becomes what it is in practice -- a piece of leased ground with a maintenance
schedule, an invoice, a named holder, and a public face.

The boundary with what khai already carries is clean. The internal bond a mourner carries forward, and
the oscillation between confronting an absence and rebuilding around it, is **grief**'s; this engine
reads the plot and not the mourning. The rite that puts a body in the ground is **ritual**'s. The object
withheld from exchange to carry a lineage is **heirloom**'s, the possession an identity has extended
into is **extended-self**'s, and the thing a collective lodges its we in is **totem**'s. The record an
institution keeps of a person is **document**'s, and the shared ground that costs somebody to keep is
the **upkeep** composite's and **commons**'s.

**The place** is somewhere else by design. The dead were moved out of the parish yard onto managed land
at the edge of the settlement, and what a household buys there is usually a right of burial for a term
rather than the earth -- the only property most people hold that they pay for, visit, maintain, and can
neither occupy nor sell. **What it holds up** is that this person belonged to somebody: a body in
unmarked ground has been disposed of, and a body under a name has been kept. **What is apparent** is a
name, two dates, a line priced by the letter, and a container of flowers in one of three conditions --
and passers-by read the plot before they read the stone.

**The next of kin** holds a right, an invoice, and the phone number on the file. The powers are small
and absolute: who else may be put in, whose name is added, what the wording says. What is lost is the
privacy of the holder's own mourning, since grief that would otherwise be nobody's business is on
display in the form of groundskeeping -- and eventually the right itself, when a renewal notice reaches
an address twenty years out of date.

**The burying** fixes an address at the worst possible moment for choosing one, on cost and on what was
available that Thursday, binding a family to a town none of them may still live in. **The visiting** is
mostly work: the ethnographies find weeding, scrubbing, refilling, tidying the neighbouring plot, and
talking aloud, and the tasks are what makes the visit possible, since a person who arrives with nothing
to do stands at a stone for four minutes and leaves. **The lapsing** is an ending arrived at by a
sequence of reasonable omissions, and the object records only the sum.

Hence the **twist**, in the anchor's Yearbook. **A grave is the only part of a death anybody can still
maintain, which is why it is the part the living are judged on.** Nothing else about a death can be
tended: the loss cannot be worked on, the person cannot be helped, and the feeling cannot be shown to
anybody at a distance. The plot can be weeded, and the weeding is visible. So the one available task
becomes the measure, and a family grieving hard two hundred miles away keeps a worse grave than a family
grieving little who live nearby.

The engine is built so that nobody has to be neglectful for any of it to run, and a play should keep it
that way. Nobody neglected it. Nobody decided to stop. The visits thinned and the years did the rest.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This is a history-and-ethnography engine with no experimental base
at all, and the reader should not be left to discover that. **Laqueur's thesis is a historical argument,
not a measurement.** That the work of the dead is done for the living and is civilisationally deep is
magnificently documented across two thousand years of practice, and no study bears on it; it is the kind
of claim that is established by showing rather than by testing. **The visiting movement rests on a small
number of deep local studies.** The tending, the talking aloud, the fixed route and the care of a
neighbouring plot come from close ethnographic work in a handful of cemeteries in one city, and they are
persuasive precisely because they are close -- which is also why they should not be read as a general
account of how people visit graves. **Practice varies enormously by religion and region and the engine
flattens it.** Stones left instead of flowers, graves visited once and then never, obligatory annual
tending on a fixed date, exhumation and reburial after a set term, and elaborate collective care of
plots by people unrelated to the dead are all normal somewhere, and this engine's default -- irregular
individual visiting by close kin, thinning over years -- is a Northern European and North American
pattern presented as the shape of the thing. **The engine assumes there is a grave.** In much of the
world most deaths now end in cremation, often with no plot and no address, and the engine says nothing
about what changes when the object is absent -- which is a real gap, since the twist depends entirely on
there being something maintainable. **The judged-on claim is attested rather than measured.** That
strangers and relatives read a plot's condition as a statement about the family runs through the
historical and ethnographic material and through the entire economics of memorial masonry, and nobody
has put a number on it. **The lease and reuse arrangements are jurisdictional.** Terms of years, grants
in perpetuity, statutory reuse after a period, and the practical impossibility of tracing a
grant-holder differ so much between countries that the lapsing member states a general shape from
particular systems. What should not be taken from this engine is that visiting is neurotic, that people
who stop are failing anybody, or that the plot is a substitute for mourning. The claim is narrow: the
object is leased, the address is fixed under duress, the bond runs through maintenance, and the
maintenance is what gets read.

## Origin

twist follows from Laqueur; the account of the visit is Francis, Kellaher and Neophytou's.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The mourning itself (grief, memory, lasting)**: The adaptation to irreversible loss, the bond
  carried forward internally, and what survives of a feeling over time are those packages'. This engine
  reads the object and the tasks it issues.
- **The rite (ritual, rite of passage, ceremony)**: The funeral, the committal, the observances, and
  what a rite does to the people performing it are ritual's. The burying member starts where the rite
  ends, at the point the location becomes irreversible.
- **The object that carries an identity (heirloom, extended-self, totem, investiture)**: The thing
  withheld from exchange to carry a lineage, the possession a self has extended into, and the article a
  collective lodges its we in are those engines'. A grave is held rather than owned and cannot be moved,
  which is a different arrangement from all three.
- **The institutional record (document, legibility, register)**: The death certificate, the burial
  register, the plot number, and the state's readable index of who is where are those engines'. The
  grave is the physical position, not the entry.
- **The public memorial (the world, and Doss's territory)**: The war memorial, the roadside shrine, the
  bench with a plaque, and the national monument do work this engine does not model. They are addressed
  to a public; a grave is addressed to a family and overheard by passers-by.
- **The shared ground and who pays for it (upkeep, commons, altruistic-punishment)**: How a common
  resource is maintained, who contributes, and who enforces are those packages'. The engine reads one
  plot and the person holding it.
- **The disposal industry (the world)**: Funeral costs, plot pricing, masonry, cemetery regulation,
  exhumation licensing, and the statutory terms of reuse are matters of trade and law. They set the
  format; this engine reads what the object does to the people left holding it.
- **The grave as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A plot staged in order to **stand for** a theme --
  the headstone that mirrors a motif of memory -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's pitch.
  Grave sets what the object requires and what its condition says; the meaning read into it is the
  play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A piece engine: one anchor, one position, three processes, all flat beneath the
object.

- **[grave](piece_grave.md)** (the anchor): the marked place of the dead, leased rather than owned,
  located under duress and read by strangers, carrying the twist -- a grave is the only part of a death
  anybody can still maintain, which is why it is the part the living are judged on. Anchored by Laqueur
  (2015) and Ariès (1977).
- **[next of kin](position_next_of_kin.md)** (the position): the holder of the burial right, the
  invoice, and a set of small absolute powers over how a shared loss is presented, who loses the privacy
  of their own mourning and eventually the right itself. Anchored by Rugg (2000).
- **[the burying](process_the_burying.md)**: the day the location becomes permanent, decided in a
  fortnight on cost and availability. Anchored by Ariès (1977).
- **[the visiting](process_the_visiting.md)**: the bond carried on through small horticultural tasks,
  which are what makes the visit possible. Anchored by Francis, Kellaher & Neophytou (2005).
- **[the lapsing](process_the_lapsing.md)**: the ending assembled out of individually innocent weeks,
  which the object records only as a sum. Anchored by Rugg (2000).
