# Caregiving: Reference

## Line of Work

Caregiving as **process**: the behavioral system through which a persona is moved to protect and provide for a vulnerable other whose need is felt -- the reciprocal complement to the attachment system's seeking of a secure base. The domain does not model the persona's own reaching for care under threat, which is attachment's; nor the feeling-with that moves the care, which is empathy's; nor the discrete transfer of a resource under a norm of return, which is gift's; nor the resting depletion of a persona's self-regulatory reserves, which the executive-function and regulation engines hold. It models the disposition to answer another's need itself: the vulnerability read, the move to provide, and the shapes that providing takes.

The spine is the attachment-theoretic account of caregiving as a behavioral system. Bowlby posited caregiving as the reciprocal of attachment -- a system, roused by a vulnerable other's need or distress, that organizes protection and provision the way attachment organizes proximity-seeking. Its optimal expression is sensitive responsiveness: the accurate reading of the other's signals and a fitted answer, from which a secure base is built. It fails in two directions -- by overshooting into controlling, autonomy-denying overprotection, and by depleting the giver into burnout and compassion fatigue when demand outruns replenishment. The three forms are those: sensitivity, overprotection, burnout.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Attachment**: The engine models the giving of care, not the seeking of it. The persona's own reaching for a secure base under threat -- secure, anxious, avoidant, or disorganized -- is owned by the attachment engine; caregiving is the reciprocal system that answers that reaching, and owns the provision, not the pursuit.
- **Empathy**: The engine models the tending, not the feeling-with that moves it. The perception of and vicarious sharing in another's state is owned by the empathy engine; caregiving draws on empathy as a motivator but is the behavioral system of provision, which can run without the feeling and can be felt without acting, and owns the answering, not the affect.
- **Gift**: The engine models a standing disposition to answer need, not a discrete transfer under a norm of return. The bounded giving of a resource that invokes reciprocity is owned by the gift engine; caregiving provides for a vulnerable other without the expectation of return that defines a gift, and owns the tending, not the exchange.
- **Executive function and regulation**: The engine models the caregiver emptied by giving, not the resting reserve or its management. The persona's baseline self-regulatory capacity and the strategies that husband it are owned by the executive-function and regulation engines; burnout draws those reserves down through care, and owns that depletion-through-caring, not the reserve itself.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per file.

- **[caregiving](process_caregiving.md)** (the root): The behavioral system that answers a vulnerable other's need -- the reciprocal of attachment's reaching, the move to protect and provide roused by another's distress. Anchored by Bowlby (caregiving as a behavioral system) and George & Solomon (the caregiving system).
- **[sensitivity](process_sensitivity.md)** (form): The attuned answer -- the need read accurately and met as it is, from which a secure base grows. Anchored by Ainsworth (maternal sensitivity) and Collins & Feeney (responsive care as safe haven and secure base).
- **[overprotection](process_overprotection.md)** (form): The overshoot -- controlling, autonomy-denying care sized to the giver's fear rather than the other's need. Anchored by Collins & Feeney (over-involvement that undermines autonomy) and George & Solomon (dysregulated caregiving).
- **[burnout](process_burnout.md)** (form): The depletion -- the caregiver emptied by sustained demand, compassion worn thin, care failing from the inside. Anchored by Figley (compassion fatigue) and George & Solomon (the cost and collapse of the caregiving system).
