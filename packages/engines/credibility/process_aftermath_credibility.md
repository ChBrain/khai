# Process: The Aftermath

## Taxonomy

[Credibility](process_credibility.md), what it does to the persona reporting, and where the engine is seen for one thing.

## Owner

- Project: khai
- Engine: credibility

## Initiated by

The account was not taken, and something happens to the person who gave it.
The Aftermath starts after the hearing: [self-doubt](process_the_self_doubt.md) about their own capacity to know, [a belated](process_the_belated.md) recognition that arrives as a second injury, and [the owing back](process_the_owing_back.md), which nobody has a procedure for.

## Direction

Toward a persona altered as a knower rather than merely unheard.
The intuitive picture is of somebody who knows what happened, cannot get it across, and is waiting intact for a better listener. That is not what repeated discounting produces.
What it produces is a persona who hedges before speaking, revises the account toward whatever might be credited, checks their own memory against the doubt of people who were not there, and eventually declines to form a view at all.

## Lever

The reality of what was known against what the discounting did to the knowing.
The carrying force is that some revision is healthy and the persona is not simply being crushed: hearing doubt and re-examining one's account is how anybody corrects a mistake, most reports do get taken, and a persona who never revised under challenge would be impossible and frequently wrong; the resistance is that the revision here is driven by reception rather than by evidence, so the account converges on what is credible rather than on what happened, and the persona cannot tell the two processes apart from inside.
The Aftermath rides the engine's keystone knife-edge: being disbelieved does not leave a persona holding an unheard truth, it leaves them holding less of one.

## Echo

Somebody who no longer trusts themselves about their own afternoon.
It shows the three earlier movements complete -- a report with no second source, a discount applied before listening, a wrong with no word for it -- and adds the thing they were doing all along, which was working on the reporter rather than on the report.
This is why the Aftermath is the hardest of the four to stage and the only one worth closing on: the hearers must have been reasonable, the persona must have been right, nothing must have been done maliciously, and the audience must be able to see somebody lose their hold on something they knew.
A play that stages the hearers as dismissive has replaced the mechanism with a villain, and one that stages the persona as vindicated has replaced it with a case.
The Aftermath is dated from the second time and compounds quietly.
