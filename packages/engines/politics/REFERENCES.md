# Politics: Reference

## Line of Work

Politics as **engine**: how a persona gets an outcome out of a body that decides collectively, where
the rule for deciding is itself in play. The engine's boundary is easiest to state by what it is not.
It is not persuasion, which is the swaying composite's: the characteristic political move leaves every
preference in the room exactly as it was and changes the situation so those same preferences produce a
different result. It is not bargaining between two parties, which is dealing's. It is not rank in a
deference order, which is standing's. It is not being made compliant, which is subjection's. It is
what a persona does when the decision is not theirs and the procedure that will decide it was written
by somebody.

The founding fact is Arrow's. No rule for turning individual rankings into a collective one satisfies a
short list of obviously reasonable conditions at once, so there is no neutral way to add preferences
up and the rule is always a party to the outcome. McKelvey sharpened it into something operational:
where a question has more than one dimension, an agenda-setter free to order the votes can walk a body
to almost any outcome by a sequence of pairwise choices each of which the body genuinely prefers. This
is why the engine treats procedure as contestable rather than as furniture, and why a body with one
dimension of disagreement and a fixed neutral rule has very little politics in it -- it will mostly get
what the median wants, which is Black's result and the boundary condition on everything here.

**The three faces** come from a quarrel that ran for two decades and is the engine's spine. Dahl's
study of New Haven looked at who prevailed on decisions actually taken and found no single ruling
group -- the first face, where power is what shows in an observable contest. Bachrach and Baratz
answered that this misses the second face: the power to keep questions off the table at all, the
non-decision, which leaves no record because nothing was decided. Lukes added a third: the power to
shape what people want, so that a question is not raised because nobody has a way to want it raised.
The three are not degrees of the same thing. They differ in what counts as evidence, which is exactly
why they are still argued about, and the engine carries all three with that argument attached.

**The levers** are Riker's contribution and his word for them is the right one. Heresthetics is the art
of structuring a situation so that you win without having changed anybody's mind -- and it is a
different craft from rhetoric, with its own moves. Dimension-splitting introduces a cross-cutting
question that divides the winning coalition. Agenda-setting exploits McKelvey. Coalition-building
follows the size principle: aim at the minimum winning coalition rather than the largest, since the
spoils divide among the members and every surplus partner was paid for nothing. Schattschneider
supplies scope-shifting and the sentence the second face rests on -- the definition of the
alternatives is the supreme instrument of power -- along with the observation that every fight has an
audience, that the audience usually decides it, and that the losing party's move is therefore to make
the fight bigger. Logrolling is the trade a counting rule invites by being blind to intensity.
Stalling is built from the body's own safeguards. The rule contest is the slowest lever and the only
one whose winner keeps winning after they stop paying attention.

**The footings** are what makes a lever bite, and the veto point is Tsebelis's: a persona in a chain
where consent is required can prevent everything passing through them without being able to carry
anything, which is a structurally different position from having votes. The broker seat, the bloc, and
the mandate are the ordinary currencies of assembly. The outsider is the absence of all four, and it
is included because it is the common case: most people are affected by most bodies that decide about
them and are not part of how those bodies decide. It carries exactly one lever, scope-shifting, and
carries it fully.

The engine's twist is what a complete victory leaves behind. A win at the open contest leaves a record
of a fight; a win at the agenda leaves an absence; a win at the settled premise leaves nothing at all,
because the question was never a question. So the more completely a persona has won, the less there is
to see, and a body's quiet reads as consent to everybody in it -- including to whoever arranged it.
The best-governed-looking body is not reliably the one with the least politics in it.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This engine is built from political theory and formal results
rather than from experiment, and the parts differ sharply in what kind of thing they are. Arrow's
theorem and McKelvey's are proofs: they are not in doubt, but what they imply about real bodies is
argued, since Black's median-voter result restores stability wherever disagreement really is
one-dimensional and institutions are often built to force that condition. The three faces are
contested political theory, not findings, and the objections are serious rather than pedantic: the
second face was attacked on the ground that a non-decision leaves no observable trace, so the claim
cannot be tested and a persona alleging it cannot be distinguished from a bad loser -- a difficulty
the engine writes into the face itself. Lukes's third face draws the sharper objection, that
identifying somebody's real interests against their expressed ones requires a standard, and whoever
supplies it is doing something rather like the thing being criticised; that objection is written into
the member too, and a play should not treat the third face as a diagnosis anybody can hand down.
Riker's heresthetics is argued from worked historical cases and is illuminating rather than measured;
it should be read as a repertoire that exists, not as a claim about frequency. The size principle has
mixed empirical support -- surplus coalitions are common in real legislatures, for reasons including
insurance against defection -- and the engine states it as the counter-intuitive logic rather than as
what usually happens. Michels's iron law of oligarchy stands behind the whole engine as a
generalisation from limited cases and is treated here as a caution rather than a law. What should not
be taken from this engine is that every quiet body is being managed, or that a persona who prevails in
a committee did so by manipulation. Most decisions are what they look like. The engine's claim is that
where they are not, the alternatives are enumerable, and this is the list.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Changing somebody's mind (persuasion, swaying)**: The message, the route, and the resistance an
  attempt raises are the persuasion and reactance engines' and the swaying composite's. Politics is
  the complement: a persona who won by changing minds has run swaying, and a persona who changed no
  minds and won has run this. A play may use both, and should not describe a heresthetic move as an
  argument.
- **What a persona can move others with (power)**: Reward, coercion, legitimacy, reference, expertise,
  and information are the power engine's six bases. Politics takes a persona's bases as given and
  reads what they can do with them where a body decides; the bases are the resource, the levers are
  the play.
- **Staying, leaving, and pressing from inside (loyalty)**: Exit and voice are the loyalty engine's
  expressions, and Hirschman's triad belongs there whole. Politics does not restate it: where a
  persona's question is whether to leave or to press, that engine has it, and this one begins after
  they have decided to press.
- **A bargain between two parties (negotiation, dealing)**: Preparation, exploration, invention, and
  resolution are the negotiation engine's, and the dealing composite reads a bargain with trust and
  escalation. Politics requires a body of more than two and a rule for deciding among them, which is
  what makes coalition and agenda possible at all.
- **Rank in the deference order (hierarchy, status, standing)**: Who outranks whom, who directs and
  who answers, and the tactical moves in status space are those engines' and the standing composite's.
  A persona may be low in rank and strong in footing, or the reverse, and the engine keeps them apart.
- **Being made compliant (obedience, discipline, subjection)**: Commands obeyed against conscience,
  disciplinary power, socialization, and the total institution are those engines' and the subjection
  composite's. Politics reads a persona acting on a body; subjection reads a body acting on a persona.
- **Who pays to keep a shared thing (commons, upkeep)**: The common-pool resource, the conditional
  contribution, and the costly sanction are the commons and altruistic-punishment engines' and the
  upkeep composite's. Olson's collective-action problem sits there rather than here; politics assumes
  a body that already exists and asks how its decisions come out.
- **How a group forms and how an organisation runs (group, org, airing)**: Forming and storming, the
  competing-values stances, and whether it is safe to speak are those engines' and the airing
  composite's. Politics can run in a group at any stage and in an organisation of any culture.
- **The us/them line and who belongs (social-identity, intergroup, membership)**: Identification,
  bias, and the standing bind to a group are those composites'. A bloc is not an identity and a
  coalition is not a group: both are assembled for a question and dissolve when it is settled.
- **The contest as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A committee fight made to **stand for** a
  theme -- the vote that mirrors a motif of futility -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's
  pitch, not the force. Politics sets how the decision came out; the meaning read into it is the
  play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A process root over three faces, seven levers, and five footings.

- **[politics](process_politics.md)** (the root): how a persona gets an outcome out of a body that
  decides collectively, where the rule is in play. Anchored by Arrow (1951) and McKelvey (1976) on why
  the rule is never neutral, and bounded by Black (1958).
- **[the open contest](position_first_face.md)** (face): the visible fight on a tabled question,
  decided by numbers, leaving a record. Anchored by Dahl (1957, 1961).
- **[the agenda](position_second_face.md)** (face): the non-decision -- what never reaches the table,
  and why it leaves no evidence. Anchored by Bachrach & Baratz (1962) and Schattschneider (1960).
- **[the settled premise](position_third_face.md)** (face, the twist): the question nobody raises
  because nobody has a way to want it raised. Anchored by Lukes (1974, 2005), with his critics.
- **[agenda-setting](process_agenda_setting.md)** (lever): what is voted on and in what order.
  Anchored by McKelvey (1976) and Kingdon (1984).
- **[dimension-splitting](process_dimension_splitting.md)** (lever): a cross-cutting question that
  divides the winning coalition. Anchored by Riker (1986).
- **[scope-shifting](process_scope_shifting.md)** (lever): changing who is watching, outward for the
  loser and inward for the winner. Anchored by Schattschneider (1960).
- **[coalition-building](process_coalition_building.md)** (lever): assembling the minimum winning
  coalition and no more. Anchored by Riker (1962), with the surplus-coalition evidence against it.
- **[logrolling](process_logrolling.md)** (lever): trading support across questions where intensities
  differ and the rule is blind to intensity.
- **[stalling](process_stalling.md)** (lever): spending the clock, using the body's own safeguards.
- **[the rule contest](process_rule_contest.md)** (lever): fighting over the procedure, argued in
  general terms and applied in particular ones.
- **[the veto point](position_veto_point.md)** (footing): consent required, so everything can be
  stopped and nothing started. Anchored by Tsebelis (2002).
- **[the broker seat](position_broker_seat.md)** (footing): sitting in a gap whose width is the
  persona's whole value.
- **[the bloc](position_bloc_hold.md)** (footing): being able to deliver others, and being commanded
  by them in return.
- **[the mandate](position_mandate_claim.md)** (footing): speaking for people who are not present, at
  the cost of being unable to be personally convinced.
- **[the outsider](position_outsider_stand.md)** (footing): the absence of the other four, carrying
  scope-shifting alone. Anchored by Scott (1990).
