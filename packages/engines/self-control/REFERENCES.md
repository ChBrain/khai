# Self-Control: Reference

## Line of Work

Self-control as a **multi-type** engine: the effortful, in-the-moment override of a prepotent impulse in favor of an endorsed standard, taken across the forms the struggle takes and the trait that tunes it. The domain does not model the cold cognitive inhibition of a prepotent response for accuracy, which is the executive-function engine's; nor the valuation and discount that make a temptation pull, which is the reward engine's; nor the long-horizon passion and perseverance toward a distant goal, which is the grit engine's; nor the if-then plans that pre-empt the conflict, which is the implementation-intention engine's; nor the compulsive craving of dependence, which is the addiction engine's. It models the motivational struggle itself: an impulse the persona genuinely wants against a goal it genuinely holds, and the effort that resolves it -- or does not.

The spine is the psychology of self-regulation. Metcalfe and Mischel's hot/cool framework casts self-control as a cool, reflective system regulating a hot, impulsive one, and Mischel's delay-of-gratification paradigm -- the marshmallow test -- shows both the strategies that make waiting possible (attention deployment, abstract construal, distancing) and the long reach of early self-control across the lifespan. Baumeister, Vohs and Tice's strength model frames willpower as an effortful, limited capacity, though the ego-depletion effect at its center has faced serious replication difficulty, so the momentary "resource" is better read as the pull exceeding the hold than as a literal fuel gauge. Tangney, Baumeister and Boone establish trait self-control as a powerful individual difference predicting health, achievement, and relationships -- and later work (Hofmann; Galla and Duckworth) shows that much of the trait's advantage comes not from winning hard fights but from habits and situations that spare the persona the fight. The altitudes are those: the override, its restraint, delay, and lapse, and its trait.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Executive function**: The engine models the hot motivational override, not the cold cognitive inhibition. Executive function -- the inhibition of a prepotent response, updating, and shifting, deployed for accuracy on a task -- is owned by the executive-function engine; self-control is the struggle against an impulse the persona actually wants, in favor of a standard it endorses, which may recruit that inhibition but is the felt want-versus-ought conflict, not the cold control mechanism. The two compose: executive function is the machinery, self-control the motivational fight it may serve.
- **Reward**: The engine models the resisting, not the valuation resisted. The reward engine owns how outcomes are valued and discounted -- how much the smaller-sooner outweighs the larger-later, the hyperbolic curve that makes a temptation pull; self-control is the persona's work against that pull, the override of a value it feels, and it hands the valuation across while owning the resistance.
- **Grit**: The engine models the in-the-moment impulse override, not the long-horizon pursuit. Grit -- sustained passion and perseverance toward a distant goal over years -- is owned by the grit engine; self-control is the momentary resisting of a specific temptation, and the two are distinct, a persona can be gritty toward a life goal while lapsing on daily temptations, or resist every treat with no long project at all.
- **Implementation intention**: The engine models the effortful in-the-moment conflict, not the plan that forestalls it. If-then plans that pre-commit a response to a cue -- and so pre-empt the struggle before it arises -- are owned by the implementation-intention engine; self-control is the fight when it comes, which good implementation intentions reduce the need for. The two compose: the plan spares the fight, self-control is the fight unspared.
- **Addiction**: The engine models the ordinary, recoverable override, not the compulsive craving of dependence. Addiction -- the standing compulsion where control is overwhelmed as a condition, the craving that reorganizes wanting itself -- is owned by the addiction engine; self-control's lapse is the everyday, resumable failure of a will meeting a strong temptation, not the pathological loss of control that dependence is.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per file.

- **[self-control](process_self_control.md)** (the root, process): The effortful override of a prepotent impulse in favor of an endorsed standard -- the hot want mastered by the cool ought. Anchored by Metcalfe & Mischel (hot/cool) and Baumeister (the strength model).
- **[restraint](process_restraint.md)** (process, form): The override itself -- inhibiting the prepotent, wanted response, the effortful no said at the edge of action. Anchored by Baumeister and Hofmann (everyday temptations).
- **[delay](process_delay.md)** (process, form): Delay of gratification -- forgoing the smaller-sooner for the larger-later, and the cooling strategies that hold the interval. Anchored by Mischel (the marshmallow paradigm) and Metcalfe & Mischel (cooling).
- **[lapse](process_lapse.md)** (process, form): The failure -- the impulse winning, the standard broken, the ordinary and recoverable defeat of the will. Anchored by Hofmann (the frequency of enactment) and the ego-depletion critique.
- **[disciplined](position_disciplined.md)** (position, trait): A standing capacity to override impulse reliably, working largely through habits and situations that spare the fight. Anchored by Tangney, Baumeister & Boone (trait self-control) and Galla & Duckworth (habits).
