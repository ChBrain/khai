# Altruistic Punishment: Reference

## Line of Work

Altruistic punishment as a **process** engine: the costly sanctioning of a norm violator -- a persona spending its own resource to make a defector pay for breaking a cooperative norm, with no material return and often no personal stake -- taken across the two forms the act takes by the punisher's relation to the breach. The domain does not model the moral disapproval that motivates the sanction, which is the condemnation composite's; nor the bare mechanics of delivering harm to another who would avoid it, which is the aggression engine's; nor the foregoing of a deserved sanction, which is the forgiveness engine's; nor the institutional, rule-graduated sanction of a governed commons, which is the commons engine's. It models the individual persona's costly, norm-enforcing act itself.

The spine is Fehr's experimental economics of strong reciprocity. Fehr and Gachter showed, in public-goods games, that people will pay a personal cost to punish free-riders even when the game is one-shot and anonymous -- when punishing recovers nothing and there is no future round to protect -- and that the mere availability of such punishment sustains cooperation that otherwise decays. Because the punisher bears a cost and the benefit (a shored-up norm, deterred defection) flows to the group, they named the behaviour altruistic punishment. Fehr and Fischbacher extended it past the wronged party: in third-party punishment games, uninvolved observers spent to punish unfair allocations they merely witnessed and that cost them nothing, the sharpest evidence that the motive is the norm rather than the self. The engine takes these two as its forms -- the sanction from inside the exchange and the sanction from outside it -- and holds the defining feature, the cost borne for no material return, as the mark that separates the act from ordinary self-interest. The altitudes are those: the costly norm-enforcing sanction, and its second-party and third-party forms.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **Moral disapproval (condemnation)**: The engine models the costly act, not the feeling that motivates it. The moral emotion aimed at a violator -- the blend of anger, disgust, and contempt that marks them as blameworthy -- is owned by the condemnation composite; altruistic punishment is what the persona does about that judgment when it spends to enforce it. The one is the verdict felt; the other is the sanction paid for. A persona can condemn without punishing, and the engine begins where the resource is actually spent.
- **The mechanics of harm (aggression)**: The engine models a norm-enforcing sanction, not the general delivery of harm. Behaviour intended to harm another who is motivated to avoid it -- routed by provocation, goal, or threat -- is owned by the aggression engine; altruistic punishment overlaps it wherever the sanction is a harm, but is distinguished by what it is for (enforcing a violated cooperative norm) and what it costs (a personal price, no material return). Aggression owns the force; this engine owns the costly enforcement the force may serve.
- **The release of the sanction (forgiveness)**: The engine models the imposing of a cost on the violator, not the foregoing of it. The decision to release a transgressor from deserved punishment and let resentment go is owned by the forgiveness engine; altruistic punishment is its opposite number, the choice to spend rather than to release. The one absorbs the wrong; the other makes the wrong expensive.
- **The governed commons sanction (commons)**: The engine models an individual persona's costly act, not a standing institution. The graduated, rule-bound sanctions a self-governing commons imposes on rule-breakers -- Ostrom's design principle -- are owned by the commons engine's sanction place; altruistic punishment is the person-level behaviour that such institutions formalize and that arises even where no institution exists. The one is the rule's machinery; the other is the individual choosing to pay.
