# Hospitality: Reference

## Line of Work

Hospitality as **process**: the conversion of a stranger into a guest, and the terms the conversion
carries.

The engine's subject is an anomaly and its resolution. A stranger is a person with no place in the
local order -- not kin, not neighbour, not enemy, not client, owing nothing and owed nothing -- and
that condition is not merely unclassified but unaccountable, so it cannot be left standing. Pitt-Rivers'
argument is the spine: there are two resolutions available and only two. The stranger is taken in as a
guest, or they remain outside as a threat. The warmth at the door is the near face of a decision whose
far face is hostility, and both parties know it.

The boundary with what khai already carries is clean. The transfer that binds precisely by naming no
price is the **gift** engine's; hospitality uses gift logic and is not it, because a gift can be
declined and a guest's acceptance cannot be partial. The informal public ground where **nobody is host
or guest** is third-place's -- Oldenburg defines neutral ground by the absence of exactly this relation,
which makes it the engine's negative space rather than an overlap. The kept flame of a household is
hearth's; the person held by a place is dwelling's.

**The taking in** is performed rather than agreed. The **crossing** does the classifying: outside, a
persona is unplaceable; inside, they are a guest, protected, and bound -- which is why doorways carry so
much ceremony and why the commitment is total and immediate, made on no information at all. The
**offering** seals it, and it is nearly always something ingested or received bodily rather than a word
or an object: bread, salt, water, tea, a share of the fire. Once taken, both parties are bound and
neither can undo it. The guest has no way to accept the shelter and decline the bond, because the
offering is indivisible.

**The terms** are asymmetric and both halves are heavy. The **host's duty** is to provide unstintingly,
without visible calculation, and to protect -- in the strong versions against enemies, against kin, and
at real cost. The **guest's duty** is almost entirely negative: do not rearrange, do not invite others,
do not direct, do not comment, do not refuse. Every item protects one fact, that there is a single
authority in this house and it is not them, which is why a guest who is helpful in the wrong way
breaches as surely as one who is rude. And the **precedence** -- the better seat, the first serving, the
household's own portion given away -- is genuine honour, must be accepted, and is the engine's twist
compressed into a seating arrangement: only somebody with authority can raise another.

**The clock** is the movement the engine turns on. The **welcome** is sincere, and it is bounded, and
those two facts are not visible together: a host within the term genuinely does want the guest to stay
and genuinely is not counting, so the guest can calibrate nothing from their manner, which is exactly as
warm on the last good day as on the first. The **overstay** then changes the guest's status with no
visible signal -- provision continues because stopping would be a breach, honours continue for the same
reason, and what changes is underneath. The arrangement gives the host no legitimate way to end it, so
the only instruments are indirect, and a guest reading them correctly must pretend they did not. The
**leaving**, taken in time, converts the visit into a bond that persists and can be claimed by
children; the pressing to stay is part of the ceremony and is not a test.

**The breach** is where the arrangement's durability is explained. Harming a guest, acting as host, and
turning somebody away are all held apart from ordinary wrongs, and the framing converges across
societies with very different moralities: pollution, curse, sacrilege -- an offence against something
above the parties rather than an injury to one of them. The reason is structural. Nothing enforces
hospitality -- no contract, no court, frequently no shared language -- so its entire strength is that
breaking it is unthinkable, and the horror is the enforcement mechanism working. The cost is that a rule
held by horror cannot be argued with or applied in degrees, so it protects the arrangement against every
legitimate complaint the parties might have.

Hence the **twist**, at the root. **Hospitality honours the guest by demonstrating whose house it is.**
To give is to have; to be given to is to be, however kindly, in somebody's power. The guest is raised
and subordinated in one motion -- unable to refuse without insult, unable to reciprocate here because a
return must wait for their own house, unable to stay past the term without the honour turning into
dependence. And the more lavish the host, the more complete the demonstration, which is why the most
generous welcome can be the least comfortable to receive and why nobody can say so.

The engine is built so that nobody has to be behaving badly for any of it to run, and a play should
keep it that way. The host is genuinely generous. The guest is genuinely grateful. The arrangement is
at its most interesting when both are conducting themselves correctly and the clock runs out anyway.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This is an anthropological and philosophical engine, and its
material is ethnography, textual analysis, and argument rather than measurement -- which is a real limit
and should be read as one. **Pitt-Rivers' "The Law of Hospitality" is a structural argument**, built
largely from Mediterranean and classical material and generalised considerably further than that base
supports; it is enormously influential, it is the best single account of the mechanism, and it is not a
survey. **The near-universality of severe breach norms is the engine's most confident empirical claim
and is still a convergence rather than a measurement**: the pattern turns up wherever anybody has
looked, and nobody has counted it in a way that would let the claim be falsified. **The forms vary
enormously.** What counts as an offering, how long a term runs, who may extend hospitality, and what a
guest may touch differ so much between societies that only the skeleton travels -- and folk formulations
like the three-day rule are folklore rather than findings, so the engine states that the term is real
and unwritten rather than giving it a length. **Derrida's aporia** -- that unconditional hospitality
would abolish the host's sovereignty and therefore the hospitality, while conditional hospitality is
always already a filter -- is philosophy and is used here to sharpen the twist, not to evidence it.
**Shryock's Bedouin fieldwork** is the strongest modern demonstration that hospitality operates as a
sovereignty claim, and it is one region. What should not be taken from this engine is that hospitality
is a disguised power play, that its generosity is insincere, or that the norms described hold in the
same form everywhere. The claim is narrow: a stranger must be resolved, the resolution binds both
parties on terms nobody stated, the arrangement has a term nobody can name, and the giving is also a
demonstration.

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The transfer that binds by naming no price (gift)**: How a gift creates obligation, binds the
  parties, and requires a return is the gift engine's, and the owing composite reads the giving, the
  owed, and the settling. Hospitality uses that logic and is not it: a gift can be declined, and a
  guest's acceptance cannot be partial, because the offering is indivisible.
- **Ground with no host and no guest (third-place)**: The informal public gathering place on neutral
  ground, where attendance is voluntary and nobody is host or guest, is the third-place engine's. It is
  defined by the absence of this relation, which makes it the clean negative of this engine rather than
  a competitor.
- **The kept flame and the household (hearth, dwelling, place-attachment)**: The fire tended daily by a
  keeper is hearth's; the person held by a place is the dwelling composite's; rootedness in a place is
  place-attachment's. Hospitality reads what happens when somebody who is not held by this place is
  admitted to it.
- **The distances bodies keep (proxemics, space)**: Territory, personal distance, and the arrangement
  of a room are those engines'. The crossing is a status change effected at a boundary, not a fact
  about spacing.
- **The public self managed in interaction (face, presentation, backstage)**: Facework, impression
  management, and the region behind the performance are those engines' and composites'. A host and
  guest are both doing facework constantly and this engine reads the arrangement they are doing it
  inside.
- **Belonging to a people (membership, intergroup, social-identity)**: Who counts as one of us, and the
  relations between groups, are those engines'. A guest is precisely somebody who does not belong and is
  admitted anyway, which is a different operation from being made a member.
- **Being made to wait at somebody's door (waiting)**: Time placed at another's disposal, and the
  summons with no announced edge, are the waiting engine's. A stranger kept at a threshold is in both,
  and this engine reads only the conversion or its refusal.
- **The commercial trade that shares the word (the world)**: Hotels, restaurants, and the hospitality
  industry are a priced service between strangers with contractual terms and a stated duration. They
  borrow the vocabulary and invert the mechanism, and the engine does not model them.
- **The welcome as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A reception made to **stand for** a theme --
  the open door that mirrors a motif of grace -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's pitch.
  Hospitality sets what the taking in cost and conceded; the meaning read into it is the play's.
