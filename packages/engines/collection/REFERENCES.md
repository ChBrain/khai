# Collection: Reference

## Line of Work

Collection as **piece**: a series of objects withdrawn from the use they were made for, bounded by a
criterion that exists only in its keeper.

The engine's subject is a set with a hole in it. Baudrillard's essay on collecting is the spine, and
its argument is the one everything here hangs from: what a collector is attached to is not the objects
but the series, and a series is constituted by its missing term. The gap is therefore not a defect
awaiting repair -- it is the structure, and the item that would close it is both the object of the
whole pursuit and the end of it. Collectors do not experience this as a paradox, because they resolve
it continuously and without noticing, by widening the criterion at exactly the moment the end comes
into view.

The boundary with what khai already carries is clean. The possession experienced as a part of the self
-- the ordinary object into which an identity has extended, so that losing it diminishes the person --
is **extended-self**'s, and a collection uses that logic without being it, because what the collector
has extended into is the set rather than any item in it. The object deliberately withheld from
exchange to carry a lineage's authority forward is **heirloom**'s; a collection is withheld from _use_
and is usually quite saleable, which is the opposite arrangement. The object's material life -- worn
by handling, decayed by time, met or not by mending -- is the **object-cycle** composite's and the
**wear**, **decay**, and **restoration** engines'. And the sacred emblem that stands for a group is
**totem**'s.

**The collector** holds two things, and the objects are the smaller. The other is the criterion: what
belongs, what is a variant worth having, what is merely similar. It is theirs alone, it is not
written down anywhere that would survive them, and it is the thing being maintained. This is why the
position forecloses so much -- the objects may not be used, the series may not be finished, and the
collection cannot be honestly priced, since the figure produced by selling it is not the figure the
collector is holding.

**The hunting** is what the collection actually consists of. The search takes months and the having
takes an afternoon, so the satisfaction is delivered by the interval and charged to the object, which
is why acquisition produces a shorter and shallower pleasure than the collector expects and why the
next search begins so quickly. Danet and Katriel's observation is the useful one here: collectors
describe the chase, not the shelf.

**The completing** is where the engine is seen for one thing. As the last items come in the criterion
begins to move -- a variant is discovered, a condition threshold is revised, a date range extends, a
subseries suggests itself -- and every one of those refinements is genuine, defensible, and the
product of real knowledge the collector did not have at the start. That is precisely what makes it
work: the refinements arrive when they are needed and at no other time, and there is no vantage from
which the collector can tell an insight from a reprieve.

**The dispersal** ends it, and ends it in the one way nobody plans for. Sold, split among heirs, or
accepted by an institution that keeps six items and deaccessions the rest, the objects survive intact
and mostly keep their prices. What does not survive is the criterion, which existed nowhere but in the
collector -- so the collection does not get destroyed, it becomes unreadable, and there is no version
of the ending in which it is preserved as what it was.

Hence the **twist**, at the root. **A collection is made of what is missing from it.** The gap is what
makes a series rather than a pile, it is where the collector's attention actually rests, and closing it
would finish the thing that the collecting is for. The item that would complete the set is the one
most wanted and least wanted at once.

The engine is built so that nobody has to be unwell for any of it to run, and a play should keep it
that way. The collector is competent, absorbed, and good company on the subject. The pursuit is a
real skill. The gap stays open anyway.

**On the evidence, stated plainly.** This is a humanities engine with a thin empirical apron, and the
proportions should be visible to the reader. **Baudrillard's "The System of Collecting" is
philosophy**, essayistic and unfalsifiable as written, and the engine takes its structural claim -- the
series is constituted by its missing term -- while declining the rest of it, including the argument
that the collection substitutes for a love object and that its true final term is the collector's own
death. Those are readings, not findings, and the engine does not need them. **Danet and Katriel's
study is the closest thing here to evidence for deferred closure**: they document the strategies by
which collectors keep a series open, and it is a qualitative analysis of interviews and observation
from a modest sample, not a measurement, and nobody has since tested the claim at scale. **Pearce's
typology** -- souvenir, fetishistic, systematic -- is descriptive, its categories have soft edges, and
collectors move between them; the engine uses it for range rather than for structure. **Muensterberger
is the weakest thing on this page and is included only because it is unavoidable in the literature**:
it is clinical anecdote from a psychoanalytic practice generalised to collectors at large, its central
claim about early deprivation has never been tested against a comparison group, and no member here
rests on it. **Belk's work on collecting sits mostly inside the extended-self engine**, which owns the
possessions-and-self mechanism; this engine borrows it and hands it back rather than restating it.
What should not be taken from this engine is that collecting is pathological, that collectors are
compensating for something, or that collecting and hoarding are the same phenomenon -- the clinical
literature separates them on the criterion, the discard behaviour, and the impairment, and this engine
models only the first of the two. The claim is narrow: the criterion is the holding, the search is the
activity, the gap is the structure, and the ending is a loss of legibility rather than of value.

## Origin

The theory and study of collecting, wired as one piece with a keeper and three fates. The twist is
Baudrillard's; the deferred-closure evidence is Danet and Katriel's.

| Source                           | Key Work                                                                           | Scope                                                                                                                                                                         |
| :------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Jean Baudrillard**             | _Le Système des objets_ (1968), "The System of Collecting"                         | **The twist.** The collector is attached to the series rather than to its objects, and a series is constituted by its missing term. The anchor and the completing.            |
| **Brenda Danet & Tamar Katriel** | _"No Two Alike: Play and Aesthetics in Collecting"_ (Play & Culture, 1989)         | The strategies by which collectors pursue closure and systematically defer it, and the primacy of the chase over the holding. The hunting and the completing.                 |
| **Walter Benjamin**              | _"Unpacking My Library"_ (1931)                                                    | The object rescued out of use into the collector's circle, and ownership as the most intimate relation one can have to things. The anchor's Place and the collector position. |
| **Susan M. Pearce**              | _On Collecting: An Investigation into Collecting in the European Tradition_ (1995) | The range of the practice -- souvenir, fetishistic, and systematic collecting -- and the collection as a private classificatory order. The collector's criterion.             |
| **Werner Muensterberger**        | _Collecting: An Unruly Passion_ (1994)                                             | The psychoanalytic account of the collector's compulsion. Clinical anecdote, listed because it is unavoidable in the literature; no member rests on it.                       |

## Restrictions

What the engine refuses to model, and to whom it delegates.

- **The possession experienced as part of the self (extended-self)**: An object into which a persona's
  identity has extended, so that having it enlarges the self and losing it diminishes it, is the
  extended-self engine's, along with the appropriation and dispossession that run on it. A collection
  uses that logic and is not it, because what has been extended into is the series rather than any item
  in it.
- **The object withheld from exchange for a lineage (heirloom)**: The thing kept out of the market to
  carry a line's identity and authority across generations is the heirloom engine's. A collection is
  withheld from use rather than from sale, and is usually quite saleable, which is the opposite
  arrangement.
- **The object's material life (object-cycle, wear, decay, restoration)**: Degradation by use,
  degradation by time, and the mending that meets or fails to meet them are those packages'. The
  collection engine reads condition only as a term in the criterion.
- **The emblem that stands for a group (totem)**: A sacred object carrying a collective's identity is
  the totem engine's. A collection stands for one person and answers to nobody.
- **Wanting, rarity, and compulsion (desire, scarcity, addiction, self-control)**: Appetite for an
  object, rarity as a cue that raises value, and the compulsion that overruns a persona's own intent
  are those engines'. The engine models a pursuit that is skilled and absorbing, and it does not model
  hoarding, which the clinical literature separates from collecting on the criterion, the discard
  behaviour, and the impairment.
- **Taste as a marker of position (capital, status, differentiation)**: A collection is legible as
  distinction and frequently functions as it, and that reading belongs to those engines. This one
  reads the series and its gap.
- **Acquiring by exchange (gift, debt, money, negotiation)**: How a transfer binds, what it obliges,
  what a thing costs, and how a price is reached are those engines'. The hunting stops at the finding.
- **The market that trades in it (the world)**: Auction houses, dealers, grading services, and price
  guides are an industry with its own rules. They supply the field the hunting runs in and are not the
  operation.
- **The collection as authored symbol (the play's pitch)**: A series assembled to **stand for** a theme
  -- the one missing item that mirrors a motif of lack -- is refused: that is authored key, the play's
  pitch. The collection sets what the keeping cost and withheld; the meaning read into it is the play's.

## Encoding

Source to constraint. A piece with a keeper and three fates, hanging flat beneath the object.

- **[collection](piece_collection.md)** (the anchor): a series of objects withdrawn from use and
  bounded by a criterion only its keeper can state, carrying the twist -- a collection is made of what
  is missing from it. Anchored by Baudrillard (1968) and Benjamin (1931).
- **[the collector](position_collector.md)**: who keeps it, holding a jurisdiction and a want list,
  and foreclosed from using the objects, finishing the series, or pricing it honestly. Anchored by
  Pearce (1995) and Benjamin (1931).
- **[the hunting](process_hunting.md)**: the activity the collection actually consists of, delivering
  its satisfaction by the search and charging it to the object. Anchored by Danet & Katriel (1989).
- **[the completing](process_completing.md)**: the criterion widening exactly as the end comes into
  view, where the engine is seen for one thing. Anchored by Baudrillard (1968) and Danet & Katriel
  (1989).
- **[the dispersal](process_dispersal.md)**: the ending that leaves every object intact and destroys
  the only thing that made them a set. Anchored by Pearce (1995) and Belk (1995).
