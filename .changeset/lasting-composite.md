Add the lasting composite, whether anything of a good thing survives its arrival. Wires three atoms -- the savoring engine (the attention that makes a pleasure fuller and longer, or kills it), the capitalization engine (the reception a persona's good news meets when it is told), and the hedonic-adaptation engine (the return to the level held before, which runs whether or not anybody does anything) -- and reads the seam the three make: a good thing does not keep itself. Staged as three bridges: the attending (the notice paid before anyone else is involved -- the narrowing that lets a good fill the field, the banking of it as a memory sharpened by its own brevity, and the kill-joy turn that hunts the flaw and thins it, one lever thrown either way), the telling (the good carried outward, the strongest single amplifier there is, and then out of the persona's control: only the active-constructive reply builds the bond, while the steady nod makes nothing of it, the deflation dressed as realism is felt more sharply than absence, and the turning-away teaches the sharer not to bring the next one -- and no reply is neutral), and the fading (the twist -- the response worn down by repetition, the standard climbing exactly as fast as the persona advances, the dispositional level reclaiming them). Carries the keystone: what returns a persona from the worst is what takes the best away -- a feeling is a reaction to change, and a change that becomes the constant stops being one, so the baseline is not a flaw in the machinery but the machinery working; it simply does not know which direction anybody wanted, and takes the windfall back with the same patience it uses on the wound. Wires at Persona/Projection (audit). Anchored in Bryant on savoring as the perceived-control counterpart to coping, Bryant and Veroff's ways of savoring and kill-joy thinking, Langston's naming of capitalization, Gable, Reis, Impett and Asher's responder typology, Brickman and Campbell's hedonic treadmill, the Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman lottery-winners-and-accident-victims symmetry the keystone rests on, and Diener, Lucas and Scollon on the setpoint being real but not immovable. Distinct from mood (the feeling itself), temperament (the height of the baseline), resilience (the softening of what hurts), reward (the weighing of an outcome by its distance), addiction (needing ever more), and bias (comparison against other people rather than one's own past).
